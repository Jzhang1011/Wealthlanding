Buying a Primary Home

A clear process beats a rushed offer. Compare loan offers on official disclosures, verify the property’s risks, and leave room for the years after you move in.

Short answer

Shop lenders with written Loan Estimates, then confirm the Closing Disclosure matches what you accepted. Keep inspection, appraisal, and financing contingencies unless you knowingly price the risk. Check flood maps, insurance quotes, tax reassessment rules, HOA reserves, and how long you expect to stay — especially if retirement is within a decade.

Decision framework

You are choosing a place to live and a financing contract. Separate those workstreams: property diligence (condition, title, hazards, HOA, taxes, insurance) and loan diligence (rate, fees, cash to close, payment shock). For ages 45–65, add a third: will this house still fit healthcare access, stairs, and cash-flow needs when paychecks stop?

Mortgage shopping with official forms

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s owning-a-home tools explain the two forms that make apples-to-apples comparison possible.

Loan Estimate

Received after you apply. Compare loan amount, rate, monthly principal and interest, estimated taxes/insurance, origination charges, services you can shop, cash to close, APR, and TIP. Ask about rate lock timing. Use the CFPB Loan Estimate explainer while you review.

Closing Disclosure

You generally must receive it at least three business days before consummation. Compare it line by line to the Loan Estimate. Question changes in fees, cash to close, or loan terms. Use the CFPB Closing Disclosure explainer.

What “lowest rate” can hide

Points, lender credits, adjustable-rate features, prepayment penalties, and balloon payments change the deal. If a feature is unfamiliar, pause and read the CFPB definitions before you sign.

Property diligence checklist themes

Inspections

General inspection plus targeted reviews (roof, sewer, pests, structural) as needed. Waiving inspection is a priced risk, not a badge of strength.

Title and disclosures

Review title commitments, seller property disclosures, and HOA documents. Understand easements, liens, and litigation notes with your advisors.

Flood and hazard risk

Check FEMA flood maps and ask insurers about flood, wind, and wildfire pricing. Mortgage escrow for hazard insurance is not the same as flood coverage.

Taxes and reassessment

A purchase can reset assessed value under local rules. Confirm with the local assessor how taxes may change after sale — do not assume the seller’s bill continues.

Insurance quotes before offer

Some roofs, older electrical, or hazard zones are expensive or hard to insure. A quote can kill a deal more honestly than surprise escrow after closing.

HOA reserves and assessments

Read budgets, reserve studies, meeting minutes, and special-assessment history. Underfunded reserves are future bills.

Stay length, repairs, and retirement cash

Short stays struggle to recover transaction costs. If you expect to retire or relocate within a few years, model selling costs and a weaker market — not only today’s list price.

Budget immediate repairs separately from the down payment. Near retirement, prioritize cash reserves and healthcare access (proximity to doctors, fewer stairs, reliable transit) alongside school-district habits from earlier life stages.

Practical checklist

  • Get Loan Estimates from more than one lender for the same loan type and purchase price.
  • Compare Closing Disclosure to Loan Estimate before you wire funds.
  • Complete inspections; negotiate repairs or credits with eyes open.
  • Pull FEMA flood information and insurance quotes early.
  • Verify tax and HOA documents; ask about recent or planned special assessments.
  • Rehearse cash to close and 3–6+ months of reserves after move-in.
  • Write your expected stay length and exit plan before you waive contingencies.

Watch out for

  • <strong>Waiving inspection or financing contingencies to “win”</strong> without pricing the risk in cash reserves.
  • <strong>Shopping lenders on rate alone</strong> without comparing total fees and cash to close on the Loan Estimate.
  • <strong>Assuming the seller’s property tax bill continues</strong> after reassessment.
  • <strong>Skipping flood and insurance checks</strong> until after you are emotionally committed.
  • <strong>Buying a house that only works while both incomes continue</strong> if retirement is near.
