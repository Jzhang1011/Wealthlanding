Investment Property Realities

A rental is an operating business with leverage. Underwrite cash flow after vacancies and capex — not a headline cap rate or a stock-like return promise.

Short answer

Start from realistic rent net of vacancy, repairs, capex, property management, taxes, insurance, HOA, and utilities you pay. Cap rate and NOI describe the property before financing; your cash flow after debt service can be much thinner. Leverage amplifies gains and losses. Federal fair housing rules apply to housing providers. Tax rules for depreciation and passive losses are detailed in IRS Publications 527 and 925 — and the primary-home sale exclusion does not automatically apply to investment property.

NOI vs cash flow after financing

Two different meters

Net operating income (NOI) is generally income minus operating expenses before mortgage payments. Capitalization rate is often NOI divided by price — useful for comparing properties, not a personal return promise. Cash flow after financing subtracts principal and interest (and sometimes ignores principal as “savings”). A property can show a neat cap rate and still produce thin or negative monthly cash after debt service, capex, and vacancies.

Build a conservative rent budget

Vacancy and turnover

Empty months, cleaning, leasing fees, and concessions are normal. Underwriting at 100% occupancy is a common failure mode.

Repairs vs capital expenses

Broken appliances differ from a roof or HVAC replacement. Fund a capex reserve on purpose.

Management and your time

Self-managing is labor. Professional management is a fee. Count one of them.

Taxes, insurance, HOA, utilities

Landlord insurance differs from homeowners policies. Some HOAs restrict rentals. Confirm before you buy.

Leverage, concentration and exit

Mortgage leverage can improve returns when rents and values cooperate; it can force a sale when vacancies, rate resets, or repairs hit together. One property in one neighborhood is concentrated risk compared with a diversified fund.

Exit liquidity is slower than selling an index fund. Model selling costs, capital gains, and depreciation recapture concepts with a tax professional before you assume a clean payday. There is no guaranteed return.

Tax themes to verify with primary sources (not DIY conclusions)

These are orientation points — confirm against current IRS publications and your own facts.

Depreciation

Residential rental buildings are generally depreciated over a recovery period set in tax rules; land is not depreciated. Depreciation lowers taxable income now and reduces basis, which can increase gain on sale. See Publication 527.

Passive loss limits

Rental activities are generally passive. Losses often cannot offset wage income unless an exception applies (including possible active-participation allowances subject to income limits, or real-estate-professional rules). See Publications 527 and 925 and Form 8582 instructions.

Sale and recapture themes

Gain on investment property is not shielded by the automatic main-home exclusion. Special rate caps can apply to unrecaptured depreciation on real property — see Topic 409. Get transaction-specific advice.

§121 is for a main home

The $250,000 / $500,000 exclusion is about your principal residence with ownership and use tests — not a promise for rentals. Converting use over time has detailed rules in Publication 523.

Landlord rules without nationwide legal claims

Federal fair housing law prohibits discrimination based on protected characteristics in most housing transactions — see HUD’s Fair Housing Act overview. Security deposits, eviction procedure, rent control, and habitability standards are often state or local. Do not assume a single U.S. rulebook; read your jurisdiction and consult local counsel for leases.

Practical checklist

  • Underwrite rent at less than 100% occupancy; include turnover costs.
  • Separate operating repairs from multi-year capex reserves.
  • Compute NOI and cash flow after debt service as two different numbers.
  • Confirm insurance, HOA rental rules, and local landlord-tenant requirements.
  • Read IRS Publications 527 and 925 before relying on “paper losses.”
  • Plan the exit: selling costs, taxes, and time on market — no return promise.

Watch out for

  • <strong>Cap rate as a personal yield promise.</strong> It ignores your financing and capex.
  • <strong>Assuming §121 excludes rental gain.</strong> Main-home rules are different.
  • <strong>Ignoring passive loss limits</strong> when marketing tax benefits.
  • <strong>Underestimating vacancies, special assessments, or insurance spikes.</strong>
  • <strong>Concentration risk</strong> — one leveraged property vs diversified invested assets.

Primary sources

  • IRS Publication 527, Residential Rental Property — Rental income, expenses, depreciation, passive loss overview
  • IRS Publication 925, Passive Activity and At-Risk Rules — Passive activity limits and rental exceptions
  • IRS Topic No. 409, Capital gains and losses — Long-term vs short-term rates; personal-use loss not deductible
  • IRS Topic No. 701, Sale of your home — $250,000 / $500,000 exclusion, ownership and use tests
  • IRS Publication 523, Selling Your Home — Eligibility, basis, reporting, partial exclusion
  • HUD: Fair Housing Act overview — Federal fair housing rules for housing providers
  • FHFA House Price Index — Repeat-sales index; prices rise and fall by place and time

Rules and figures can change. Confirm on the linked pages before you act.
