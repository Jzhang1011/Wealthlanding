When Daily Support Outgrows the House

Start from what help is needed each day — not from a birthday. Independent living, assisted living and skilled nursing solve different problems, and Medicare usually does not pay for long-term custodial care.

FrameNeeds not age aloneSettingsHome → AL → SNF different jobsMedicareLimited custodial care generally not covered

Short answer

If bathing, dressing, medications, mobility or safety needs exceed what the household and home can sustain, compare settings by the support they provide: staying home with services, assisted living, or a nursing home/skilled nursing facility. National Institute on Aging materials describe these facility types. Medicare generally does not cover long-term custodial care; it may cover limited skilled care under strict conditions. Age 70+ is not, by itself, a reason to move to a nursing home.

Settings at a glance (definitions from official aging/Medicare education)

Home with services

Remain at home with family help, paid aides, adult day services or home-delivered supports. Feasibility depends on safety, caregiver capacity and money — not on optimism alone.

Independent living / retirement communities

Housing aimed at older adults with limited or no hands-on personal care. Useful when the house is the problem but daily ADLs are still manageable.

Assisted living

Per NIA, assisted living is generally for people who need help with daily care but not as much help as a nursing home typically provides. Services and regulation vary by state; Medicare does not pay for assisted living as a long-term custodial setting.

Nursing home / skilled nursing facility

Per NIA, nursing homes provide a wider range of health and personal care with more medical focus than most assisted living. Medicare may cover short-term skilled nursing under conditions after a qualifying hospital stay; it generally does not cover long-term custodial nursing home care.

Medicare and long-term custodial care

Medicare.gov states that Medicare does not pay for long-term care (custodial care / long-term services and supports) when that is the care you need. Most long-term help with activities of daily living is non-medical. People may pay out of pocket, use Medicaid if eligible under state rules, or use private long-term care insurance if they have it.

Do not plan a nursing-home stay on the assumption that Medicare will fund years of custodial care. Confirm current rules on Medicare.gov and with the facility’s billing staff.

Money links back to housing

Selling a main home can free equity (subject to selling costs and possible capital gain after any §121 exclusion). That cash may fund care — or be spent too fast if the setting is a mismatch. Coordinate housing timing with a care assessment rather than rushing a sale under crisis conditions when you can avoid it.

Practical checklist

  • List the daily tasks that need help now vs within 12 months (bathing, dressing, meals, meds, mobility, cognition/safety).
  • Ask a clinician or geriatric care manager whether home-with-services is still safe.
  • Compare assisted living vs nursing facility services using NIA overviews and local licensing rules.
  • Price the setting without assuming Medicare pays for long-term custodial care; read Medicare.gov long-term care pages.
  • If selling a home, run net proceeds and §121 eligibility before committing the money to care.
  • Prefer planned transitions over emergency placements when health allows.

Watch out for

  • <strong>Using age alone</strong> (“turned 70”) as the reason for a nursing home.
  • <strong>Assuming Medicare covers long-term custodial care.</strong>
  • <strong>Collapsing assisted living and skilled nursing into one “facility” idea.</strong>
  • <strong>Selling the house in a crisis</strong> without a care and cash-flow plan.
  • <strong>Ignoring state licensing and contract fine print</strong> for residential care settings.
