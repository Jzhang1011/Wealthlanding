Start Investing Early

Investing in your 20s is a cheat code for wealth due to compound interest. Step 4 focuses on securing your employer 401(k) match, opening a Roth IRA, and buying simple, low-cost index funds.

The Power of Compound Interest

Compound interest is the snowball effect of your money making money, and then those earnings making more money. Every dollar you invest at age 25 is worth significantly more at retirement than a dollar invested at age 35, purely because it has 10 more years to multiply.

The Investment Order of Operations

  1. The 401(k) Match: If your employer offers a match (e.g., they match up to 5% of your salary), contribute exactly enough to get it. This is a 100% immediate return on your money.
  2. The Roth IRA: Next, fund a Roth IRA. In your 20s, your tax bracket is likely lower than it will be later in life. Money goes into a Roth IRA after taxes, grows tax-free, and is pulled out tax-free in retirement.
  3. Max the 401(k): If you still have money to invest after maxing the Roth IRA, go back and increase your 401(k) contributions.

What Actually Goes Inside the Accounts?

Opening an IRA is like opening a bucket. You still have to put something in it. For 90% of people, the best strategy is buying a low-cost, broad-market Index Fund or Target Date Fund (e.g., S&P 500 index). This provides instant diversification across hundreds of companies.

Key facts and official sources for Step 4

Contribution limits and fees decide how much of your money actually gets to compound. Here are the official figures and the questions to ask.

2026 contribution limits (IRS)

  • 401(k), 403(b), most 457 plans and the Thrift Savings Plan: $24,500 of employee contributions.
  • Traditional and Roth IRAs combined: $7,500.
  • Roth IRA eligibility phases out at higher incomes.

Source: IRS announcement IR-2025-111. Limits are adjusted for inflation most years, so check the current year's figures.

Fees compound too

A fund's expense ratio is charged every year on your whole balance, so small differences add up over decades. The SEC's investor education site explains fund fees and has a free compound interest calculator at investor.gov. Before choosing a fund in your 401(k), compare expense ratios in the plan's fee disclosure.

Example

Illustrative: if your employer matches 100% of contributions up to 5% of a $60,000 salary, contributing $3,000 a year brings in another $3,000 from your employer. Check your plan's summary for the exact match formula and any vesting schedule, which can require you to stay a number of years before the match is fully yours.
