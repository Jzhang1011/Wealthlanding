How Much House Can I Afford?

The bank’s maximum is a credit decision. Your budget is the payment that still funds retirement savings, reserves, and a life you can sustain if income dips.

Housing screen~28% of gross (rule of thumb)All debts~36% ceiling screenReality checkAll-in PITI + HOA + upkeep

Short answer

Start from the monthly housing cost that leaves your savings plan intact, not from the pre-approval letter. A common planning screen is about 28% of gross income for the full housing payment and about 36% for all debt payments — use the lower binding ceiling, then subtract maintenance and keep cash reserves after closing. Near 45–65, also ask whether the payment still works if you retire earlier, lose a bonus, or need to keep maxing catch-up contributions.

Lender approval is not affordability

Two different questions

Lenders underwrite to their guidelines and secondary-market rules. They do not optimize your retirement date, college cash flow, or sleep. Treat the pre-approval as a ceiling on access to credit, then set a lower household budget that survives maintenance shocks and income variability.

Build the all-in housing number

PITI

Principal, interest, property taxes, and homeowners insurance. Taxes and insurance often change after purchase — ask how the escrow was estimated.

HOA / condo fees

Count the full monthly assessment. Review reserve studies and recent special assessments before you treat the fee as stable.

PMI or other mortgage insurance

If your down payment is under 20% on many conventional loans, budget mortgage insurance until you can remove it under the loan’s rules.

Maintenance and capex reserve

A common planning placeholder is about 1% of home value per year for repairs and replacements — label it as an assumption and raise it for older homes.

Closing funds and post-closing reserves

Down payment, closing costs, initial escrow, moving, immediate repairs, and 3–6 months of essential expenses still in cash after you close (more if income is variable).

What changes the answer?

Other debts

Heavy car or student loans can make the 36% side bind first. Paying those down can raise housing room more than a small raise.

Taxes, insurance, HOA

Same price, different payment across states and carriers. Reassessments after a sale can jump the tax bill — check local rules.

Retirement savings rate

A payment that crowds out 401(k)/IRA catch-ups at 50+ is expensive even if the lender approves it.

Income risk

Commission, business, or dual-income households should budget from a conservative base, not a record year.

Years to retirement

A 30-year loan taken at 58 still has payments deep into retirement unless you plan a sale, refinance, or payoff path.

Age and condition of the house

Roof, HVAC, foundation, and deferred maintenance are part of affordability, not surprises after closing.

Practical checklist

  • Write the all-in monthly number: PITI + HOA + PMI + maintenance reserve.
  • Compare that number to both the 28% and 36% screens; use the lower binding limit as a ceiling, not a target.
  • Keep retirement contributions (including catch-ups if eligible) in the budget before you stretch the house.
  • Confirm cash to close and 3–6+ months of reserves remaining after closing.
  • Stress-test: job loss, bonus at zero, one spouse retired, higher insurance/tax escrow.
  • Get insurance quotes and tax estimates before you fall in love with a listing.

Watch out for

  • <strong>Buying at the pre-approval max.</strong> That is the lender’s risk limit.
  • <strong>Forgetting maintenance and special assessments.</strong> Escrow does not cover the roof.
  • <strong>Draining cash for the down payment.</strong> House-rich and reserve-poor is fragile.
  • <strong>Counting on future raises or a hot market refinance.</strong> Buy what today’s sustainable income supports.
  • <strong>Ignoring retirement date math.</strong> Large payments plus early retirement is a common squeeze.
