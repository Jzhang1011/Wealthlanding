Am I Behind Financially?

Benchmarks tell you the pace you're on. They don't grade your life — or decide your next move.

Short answer

A common Fidelity benchmark is about 1× salary saved by 30 and 3× by 40, in retirement accounts only. It is a compass, not a grade. Home equity and debt change the picture. If you are behind, raising the savings rate matters more than the multiple.

The decision

The real decision isn't really “am I behind?” — it's “what's my actual position, and what closes the gap?” That takes three moves. First, measure yourself against the benchmark. Second, adjust for your real circumstances: debt, home equity, a late career start, or a partner's savings all change what the multiple means. Third, pick the savings rate that moves you forward from here.

Benchmark → personal circumstances → next action. The benchmark tells you the pace; your circumstances tell you what the pace means; the savings rate is the lever you actually control.

The simple rule — a rule of thumb, not a law

Save 15% of gross income, including the employer match. Catching up? Aim for 20% or more. The savings rate is the variable you control — and over decades it matters more than the balance you've accumulated so far.

Worked example

Say you're 35, earn $100,000, and have $80,000 saved — that's 0.8× salary, below the 1×-by-30 pace.

Saving 15% ($15,000/year) with 7% average annual returns for 25 years:
FV = $15,000 × [((1.0725) − 1) / 0.07] ≈ $949,000

Raise the rate to 20% ($20,000/year) and the same math gives ≈ $1,265,000.

The gap between “behind” and “on track” is about five percentage points of savings rate — not a lottery ticket.

Illustrative math, not personalized advice. Your numbers will differ.

What changes the answer?

High-interest debt

Net worth is assets minus debts. $80,000 saved alongside $60,000 at 22% APR is barely positive — kill the expensive debt before grading yourself.

Home equity

It counts toward net worth, but you can't spend it without selling or borrowing. Treat it as a cushion, not a retirement plan.

A late start

The benchmark assumes saving since 25. Started at 32? You're running a shorter race — the fix is a higher rate, not guilt.

Household picture

Two incomes mean two 401(k)s and two IRAs of room. Measure the household, not just your account.

Pension or equity comp

A pension, RSUs, or expected inheritance change the target. The multiple is a default for people with none of those.

Watch out for

  • Comparing yourself to averages, not medians. A few huge balances pull the average up; the median is the honest comparison.
  • Treating the multiple as a grade. It's a pace marker. Shame doesn't compound — contributions do.
  • Raiding retirement to “catch up” elsewhere. Cashing out to fund a house down payment usually moves you backward.
  • Waiting for a higher income to start. Time in the market beats timing the perfect salary; start at the match today.
