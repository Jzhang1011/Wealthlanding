🧾 Max the Tax Code

In your peak earning years, taxes are your biggest lifetime expense — bigger than housing. Every dollar you shield from taxes is a dollar that compounds for decades. Tax planning in your 30s is worth more than stock picking.

Questions this step answers

  • Roth or traditional 401(k) — which one?
  • How do I legally pay less in taxes?
  • What is an HSA and should I actually use one?
  • Backdoor / mega backdoor Roth — do I qualify?

Why this matters

Most people spend hours researching investments to squeeze out an extra 0.5% of return — and zero minutes on tax strategy that could save them 20–30% on every dollar invested. The tax code hands you legal shelters; this step is about using all of them, in the right order.

The framework

The tax pyramid

Every account is a deal with the IRS. The fewer times you're taxed, the higher the layer — fund top-down.

The HSA is the only account taxed zero times when used for medical costs — deductible in, tax-free growth, tax-free out. At 65 it becomes a traditional IRA. 529s skip the federal deduction but grow and withdraw tax-free for education (many states add a deduction).

The funding order, top to bottom:

  1. HSA to the max (if you have a qualifying high-deductible plan) — the crown jewel. Pay medical bills from cash, save receipts, let it compound.
  2. 401(k) to the full employer match — free money beats tax optimization every time.
  3. Split Roth vs. traditional by the rate rule: current marginal rate lower than expected in retirement → favor Roth; higher → favor pre-tax. Most 30s earners in high-tax states land on pre-tax 401(k) + Roth IRA. Full Roth IRA guide →
  4. Max the accounts: 401(k) to $24,500 and IRA to $7,500 for 2026, once Steps 1–3 are funded.
  5. Mega backdoor Roth if your plan allows after-tax contributions + in-service rollovers — tens of thousands more in Roth space. How it works →
  6. 529 for education goals, then taxable brokerage — no shelter left, but long-term capital-gains rates and tax-loss harvesting keep it efficient.

What's a backdoor / mega backdoor Roth — and do I qualify?

The regular backdoor Roth is for high earners locked out of direct Roth IRA contributions: make a nondeductible contribution to a traditional IRA, then convert it to Roth. There's no income limit on conversions.

The mega backdoor is bigger: some 401(k) plans let you contribute after-tax dollars beyond the normal annual limit, then roll them into Roth — potentially tens of thousands per year. You qualify only if your plan allows BOTH after-tax contributions AND in-service distributions (or in-plan Roth rollovers). Check your plan documents or ask HR — most people never realize their plan offers it. Full guide with the pro-rata trap to avoid →

What is an HSA and should I actually use one?

If you have a high-deductible health plan: usually yes. The HSA is triple tax-advantaged — deductible going in, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals for medical costs. Check the current IRS contribution limits for individual vs. family coverage.

The play: contribute the max, pay medical bills from cash, save the receipts, and let the account compound for decades.

Exception: if you have high expected medical costs, compare the HDHP's total cost against a traditional plan — the HSA doesn't win every matchup.

Do this this week

  • Confirm HSA eligibility and max it before Roth IRA
  • Split 401(k) contributions Roth vs. pre-tax using the rule above
  • Ask HR if your plan allows mega backdoor Roth
  • Harvest any taxable-account losses before year-end

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Ignoring the HSA because "it's just for medical bills."
  • Going 100% Roth at a 32%+ marginal rate.
  • Leaving an old HSA in cash instead of investing it.
