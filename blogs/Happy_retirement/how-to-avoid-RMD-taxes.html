Bill Left $6.6 Million. Tom Left $5.8 Million.
What Happened?

Illustrative case study by WealthLanding • June 2026 • Figures recalculated November 2026

Bill and Tom retired on the exact same day. For nearly thirty years, they had worked side by side as electrical engineers. They started at the same company, lived in the same neighborhood, raised their children in similar schools, and took similar vacations.

They drove similar cars. If you had attended their retirement party, you would have assumed they were living nearly identical lives. In many ways, they were. When they retired at age 60, both families had accumulated the same $3.8 million, split the same way.

Starting balances at 60 (the same for both)

  • • Traditional IRA / 401(k): $2.8M
  • • Brokerage account: $700k
  • • Roth accounts: $300k

The only difference: from 60 to 72, Bill converted part of his traditional balance to Roth each year, keeping his taxable income inside the 22% federal bracket. Tom converted nothing and took required minimum distributions from 73.

To everyone around them, the game was over. They had won. Twenty-five years later, at 85, both men had passed away. When their children settled the estates, the gap surprised them:

"After income tax, Bill's children inherited about $6.6 million. Tom's children inherited about $5.8 million. About $810,000 separated the outcomes."

The children were shocked. The families were shocked. How could two men with identical savings and identical returns leave such different legacies? Did Bill invest better? No. Did he find a secret stock? No. Did Tom make terrible choices? Also no. Tom did exactly what most retirees do. And that's the point.

The Three Decisions That Changed Everything

Bill didn't beat the market.
Bill didn't work longer.
Bill didn't take more risk.
Converted assets during low-income years
Reduced future RMD exposure
Increased tax-free wealth

The result wasn't a better portfolio. The result was a more efficient retirement system.

The Collision with Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)

The divergence showed up at age 73, when Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) began. Tom's traditional accounts had kept growing untouched, so the IRS schedule, not Tom, set how much taxable income he had to take each year. Over his lifetime Tom withdrew about $2.8 million in RMDs.

Bill paid more tax while he was converting: about $790,000 over his lifetime, against Tom's $700,000. But Bill moved about $1.7 million into Roth accounts at 22% or less, and by 85 almost nothing was left in pretax accounts. Tom still held a large traditional balance at 85. His children, in their peak earning years, would owe income tax as they withdrew it, about $1,620,000 at an assumed 32% rate. Most of the gap is that inherited tax bill.

In real life, large RMDs can also raise Medicare IRMAA premiums and make more Social Security income taxable. This illustration leaves those effects out, so it does not overstate the case.

How these numbers were calculated

Bill and Tom are illustrative. Both are married couples modeled year by year from 60 to 85 with the same WealthLanding assumptions, the same calculation used for Bill and Tom on the Retirement Blueprint page:

  • 6% yearly return on every account and 2.5% inflation.
  • $140,000 a year of after-tax spending in today's dollars, rising with inflation.
  • $60,000 a year of combined Social Security from 67, in today's dollars, with 85% treated as taxable.
  • 2026 federal brackets and standard deduction for married couples filing jointly, rising with inflation; no state tax; 15% tax on brokerage gains; half of the brokerage balance is cost basis.
  • Spending comes from the brokerage account first, then traditional accounts, then Roth.
  • RMDs from 73 using the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table.
  • Heirs pay an assumed 32% income tax on inherited traditional money; Roth and brokerage money passes without income tax. Estate tax is not modeled.

Different returns, spending or tax rates would change the result, and in some cases conversions would not pay off. Test your own numbers in the Roth conversion strategy lab. This is an educational illustration, not personalized tax or financial advice.

The WealthLanding Optimization Takeaway

Retirement isn't just a savings problem—it's an optimization problem. Two families can hold the same assets and earn the same returns, yet leave different amounts depending on when, and at what tax rate, their pretax money is taxed.
