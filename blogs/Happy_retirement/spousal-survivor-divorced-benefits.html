One Work Record, Many Checks

When one spouse did most of the paid work, the other spouse, an ex-spouse and a widow or widower may all be able to collect on that record. Here are 28 plain-language scenarios showing how spousal, survivor and divorced-spouse benefits work, with each rule checked against the Social Security Administration (SSA).

Many couples now in their 50s and 60s built their lives around one main paycheck. One spouse worked for decades; the other raised children, ran the household or worked only briefly. If that describes you, Social Security has a set of benefits designed for exactly this situation, and the rules are more generous, and more particular, than most people expect.

This guide follows one fictional couple, Frank and Linda, through 28 situations. Frank worked his whole career; Linda did not work enough to earn much of a benefit of her own. Some scenarios change the details (Linda worked a little, the couple divorced, Frank died early) so you can find the one closest to your own life.

The base case used in every calculation

Frank's primary insurance amount (PIA), the monthly benefit he would get at his full retirement age (FRA), is an illustrative $2,800. Frank and Linda were both born in 1965, so their FRA is 67. Linda has no benefit of her own unless a scenario says so. All figures are illustrative, in today's dollars, before Medicare premiums and taxes, and ignore future cost-of-living adjustments. SSA rounds actual benefits down, so real amounts will differ slightly.

One word of caution before we start: you may hear people call Social Security “SSI.” They are different programs. Supplemental Security Income (SSI) is a needs-based payment for people with little income and few resources. This guide is about Social Security retirement, spouse's and survivor benefits, which are earned through work. Scenario 5 and the box in the sidebar explain the difference.

Part A

The basics

1Who counts as a spouse

Frank and Linda married in their 20s. Their neighbors, Ray and Donna, married eight months ago, both in their 60s. Can both wives collect on their husbands' records?

The rule

To get a spouse's benefit, you generally must have been married to the worker for at least one continuous year just before you apply. That one-year requirement does not apply if you are the parent of the worker's biological child, or if you were entitled to certain Social Security benefits in the month before the marriage. SSA decides whether you are married using the law of the state where the worker lives when you apply, and in some cases treats a couple as married (a “deemed” marriage) when a ceremony was not legally valid but the applicant believed in good faith that it was. (SSA Handbook §305, §306)

Illustrative calculation
Linda: married 40 years
Meets the 1-year test
Donna: married 8 months, no child with Ray
Waits about 4 more months

Assumptions: neither wife is entitled to another benefit that would waive the one-year rule.

Watch out

The time requirements are different for each benefit: 1 year for a current spouse, 9 months for a widow or widower (with exceptions), and 10 years for a divorced spouse. Keep your marriage and divorce certificates where you can find them.

2He has to file first

Linda turns 62 and asks SSA for her spouse's benefit. Frank, 62 as well, is still working and has not applied.

The rule

A current spouse can be paid only if the worker is entitled to retirement or disability benefits, which means he has applied. The exception is for divorced spouses (Scenario 20). (SSA Handbook §305; SSA: Who can get Family benefits)

Since 2016, a worker at full retirement age can still voluntarily suspend his own payments to earn delayed credits, but SSA says that during the suspension, benefits to his spouse are suspended too. (SSA: Filing rules)

Illustrative calculation
Linda's spouse's benefit while Frank has not filed
$0
Once Frank files, if Linda is at her FRA
$1,400

Assumptions: Frank's PIA $2,800; Linda has no benefit of her own.

Watch out

“File and suspend” no longer lets a spouse collect while the worker's benefit grows. If Frank wants to wait to 70, Linda's spouse's benefit waits with him.

3Up to 50% of his full benefit, at her full retirement age

Frank files at 67. Linda, also 67, files for her spouse's benefit the same month.

The rule

The spouse's benefit is one-half of the worker's PIA. It is reduced if the spouse claims before her own full retirement age, if the family maximum applies, or if she has a smaller benefit of her own. SSA states that the maximum spouse's benefit is 50% of the amount the worker would receive at full retirement age. (SSA Handbook §320; SSA: Early or late retirement)

Illustrative calculation
Frank's benefit at 67 (100% of PIA)
$2,800
Linda's spouse's benefit at 67 (50% of PIA)
$1,400
Household total
$4,200

Assumptions: both 67; Linda has no benefit of her own; the family maximum does not bind with only two people collecting.

Watch out

The 50% is measured against Frank's full retirement age amount, not his actual check. If he claims early, his check shrinks, but Linda's spouse's benefit is still based on his PIA (Scenario 7 shows her own age reduction).

4A small record of her own: own benefit plus a top-up

Suppose Linda worked part time before the children arrived and earned a PIA of $600.

The rule

SSA always pays your own benefit first. If your spouse's benefit is higher, you get a combination of the two that equals the higher amount. Only the difference is paid as a spouse's benefit. (SSA FAQ KA-02011; Handbook §320)

Under deemed filing, for anyone who turned 62 on or after January 2, 2016, applying for either your own retirement benefit or a spouse's benefit means you are treated as applying for both, at 62 and beyond. You cannot take one and let the other grow. (SSA: Filing rules)

Illustrative calculation
Linda's own benefit at 67
$600
Spouse's top-up ($1,400 − $600)
$800
Total Linda receives
$1,400

Assumptions: both 67; Frank already filed; Linda's PIA $600. If Linda files earlier, both pieces are reduced for age; my Social Security or SSA can show the exact combined figure.

Watch out

Her own small benefit does not add to the $1,400; it fills part of it. If her own PIA were more than half of Frank's, she would get no spouse's benefit at all.

5SSI is not a spousal benefit

Linda's sister, Carol, is 66, lives alone, has $400 a month from Social Security and very little savings. A friend told her to “apply for SSI on her husband's record.”

The rule

SSI is a needs-based program for people who are 65 or older, blind or disabled and have little or no income and resources. It is not paid on anyone's work record. Social Security benefits count as unearned income for SSI, with the first $20 a month not counted. The resource limit is $2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple. When an SSI applicant lives with a spouse who is not eligible, SSA may count part of that spouse's income and resources (“deeming”). (SSA: SSI; SSI income; SSI resources)

Illustrative calculation
Carol's Social Security
$400
Minus $20 not counted
$380 countable
SSI federal amount (2027 maximum $1,029 − $380)
$649

Assumptions: Carol has no other income, meets the resource limit and lives alone. The $1,029 individual maximum is SSA's published figure for 2027; some states add a supplement. Other rules (such as help with shelter) can change the result. (SSA: SSI amounts for 2027)

Watch out

For a married woman like Linda, whose husband has a solid Social Security check, deeming usually means SSI is not available. SSI tends to matter more for a widow or divorced spouse living alone on a small benefit. And marriage itself can change or stop SSI, so report it to SSA. (SSA blog, Aug. 22, 2024)

Part B

Timing while both are alive

6Same age: she wants to claim at 62, he wants to wait to 67

Frank and Linda are both 62. Linda would like some income now. Frank plans to keep working and claim at 67.

The rule

Because a current spouse cannot be paid until the worker files (Scenario 2), Linda's claim at 62 depends on Frank filing at 62 too. Each claim is reduced for age: Frank's retirement benefit by 30% at 62 and Linda's spouse's benefit by 35% at 62, for people with an FRA of 67. (Handbook §305; SSA: Early or late retirement)

Illustrative calculation
Option 1: both claim at 62 (Frank 70% of PIA, Linda 32.5%)
$1,960 + $910 = $2,870
Option 2: both wait until 67
$2,800 + $1,400 = $4,200
Option 2 in the five years from 62 to 67
$0 from Social Security

Assumptions: base case; Frank does not earn over the earnings limit in Option 1 (see Scenario 10). Both reductions are permanent.

Watch out

If Frank claims at 62, the survivor benefit Linda could get later is also smaller (Scenario 14). The cost of claiming early is not only today's check.

7How much an early spousal claim costs

Frank has filed. Linda is deciding between 62 and 67 for her spouse's benefit.

The rule

The spouse's benefit is reduced by 25/36 of 1% per month for the first 36 months before full retirement age and 5/12 of 1% for each additional month. At 62, a spouse can receive as little as 32.5% of the worker's PIA. The reduction is permanent. The spouse's benefit does not increase after the spouse's own full retirement age. (Handbook §724; SSA: Spouse's benefit; SSA: What you could get from Family benefits)

$1,050

about $1,167

Assumptions: base case, FRA 67, Frank already filed, Linda has no benefit of her own. Reductions computed from SSA's monthly factors.

Watch out

There is no reward for Linda waiting past 67 for a spouse's benefit. Delayed retirement credits apply only to a worker's own retirement benefit.

8He waits to 70: the survivor benefit grows, the spousal benefit doesn't

Frank is healthy and decides to wait until 70. He wants to know whether that also raises Linda's check.

The rule

For people born in 1943 or later, each year of waiting past full retirement age adds 8% (2/3 of 1% per month), and the increase stops at 70. The spouse's benefit is based on one-half of the worker's PIA, so it does not include his delayed credits. A widow or widower, however, receives the same delayed-credit increase the deceased worker had earned. (SSA: Delayed retirement credits; Handbook §320; §720)

Illustrative calculation
Frank at 70 (124% of PIA)
$3,472
Linda's spouse's benefit (still 50% of PIA)
$1,400
Linda's survivor benefit if Frank dies first (at her FRA or later)
$3,472

Assumptions: base case. SSA may add some credits the January after he starts.

Watch out

If Linda is the same age, she also cannot start her spouse's benefit until Frank files at 70, three years after her own maximum at 67. Couples often weigh that gap against the larger lifetime survivor benefit. Our guide to claiming at 62, 67 or 70 walks through the trade-off.

9When ages are far apart

Change the story: Frank is 67 and Linda is 58. Or the reverse: Linda is 64 and Frank is 60.

The rule

Each person's age matters separately. A spouse must be at least 62 (unless caring for an entitled child, Scenario 11), and the worker must have filed. The early reduction depends on the spouse's own age, not the worker's. (Handbook §305; SSA: Spouse's benefit)

Illustrative calculation
Frank 67, Linda 58: Frank files now
$2,800
Linda's spouse's benefit before 62
$0
Linda at 62 (Frank 71) or at 67 (Frank 76)
$910 or $1,400
Linda 64, Frank 60: Linda's spouse's benefit before Frank turns 62 and files
$0

Assumptions: base case figures; Linda has no own benefit and no child in care.

Watch out

A much younger wife may live many years as a widow. That makes the size of Frank's benefit, and therefore the survivor benefit, especially important (Scenarios 8 and 14).

10He claims early and keeps working

Frank and Linda are both 63. Frank claimed his benefit and Linda claimed her spouse's benefit, but Frank kept consulting and expects to earn $44,480 this year.

The rule

Before full retirement age, SSA withholds $1 for every $2 earned above the annual limit, which is $24,480 in 2026. In the year a person reaches full retirement age, it withholds $1 for every $3 above a higher limit ($65,160 in 2026), counting only earnings before the month of FRA. From that month on, there is no limit. SSA has published 2027 limits of $25,560 and $67,920. (SSA: Receiving benefits while working; SSA: Exempt amounts)

The worker's excess earnings are charged against the whole family's benefits on his record, including a spouse's. A beneficiary's own excess earnings reduce only her own benefit. A divorced spouse who has been divorced at least two years is not affected by the worker's earnings. Withheld benefits are not lost: SSA recalculates the benefit at full retirement age. (Handbook §1804; 20 CFR 404.415)

Illustrative calculation
Frank at 63 (75% of PIA) + Linda at 63 (35% of PIA)
$2,100 + $980 = $3,080/mo
Earnings over the 2026 limit ($44,480 − $24,480)
$20,000
Amount withheld from the family ($1 per $2)
$10,000
Roughly equal to
3+ months of both checks

Assumptions: 2026 limits; base case; earnings are wages. SSA withholds the family's checks starting with the first months of the year until the excess is covered.

Watch out

Many couples are surprised that the worker's paycheck stops the spouse's check too. If Frank expects to earn well above the limit, waiting to claim, or confirming the effect with SSA first, avoids an unwelcome letter.

11A child under 16, or a disabled child, at home

In this version, Frank and Linda had a late-in-life child, Sam, who is 10 when Frank files at 67. Linda is 55.

The rule

A spouse of any age can get a spouse's benefit if she has in care the worker's child who is under 16 or disabled and entitled on his record. That benefit is not reduced for age. The family maximum limits the total paid to the family; the worker's own benefit is not cut, but the spouse's and children's benefits can be. Deemed filing does not apply to child-in-care spouse's benefits. (Handbook §305, §312, §320, §731; Filing rules)

For a worker who turns 62 in 2027, SSA's family maximum formula is 150% of the first $1,712 of PIA, 272% of PIA from $1,712 to $2,471, 134% from $2,471 to $3,223 and 175% above that. (SSA: Family maximum)

Illustrative calculation
Family maximum on a $2,800 PIA (worker turning 62 in 2027)
about $5,073
Frank's benefit (not reduced by the family max)
$2,800
Left for Linda and Sam ($5,073 − $2,800)
about $2,273
Each, instead of $1,400 apiece
about $1,137

Assumptions: Frank born 1965 (turns 62 in 2027); one child; no other beneficiaries. Unrounded math; SSA rounds.

Watch out

The child-in-care spouse's benefit is tied to the child's age. SSA's family benefits page describes eligibility for spouses caring for a child 15 or younger, or a disabled child of any age. When Sam turns 16, Linda's benefit on that basis generally ends; she can apply again as a spouse at 62. (SSA: Who can get Family benefits)

12He is on disability (SSDI)

Frank developed a heart condition at 58 and was approved for Social Security disability benefits. Linda is 62.

The rule

Family benefits are payable on the record of someone who is entitled to retirement or disability benefits, so the same spouse's rules apply: 62 or older, or caring for an entitled child under 16 or disabled. For a disabled worker, the family maximum is 85% of his average indexed monthly earnings, but not less than his PIA and not more than 150% of his PIA. (SSA: Who can get Family benefits; Handbook §730)

Illustrative calculation
Frank's disability benefit (his PIA)
$2,800
Linda's spouse's benefit at 62
$910
Highest possible disability family maximum (150% of PIA)
$4,200

Assumptions: base case; Linda 62, no child in care. The actual disability family maximum depends on Frank's average earnings and may be lower.

Watch out

The disability family maximum can be tighter than the retirement one, which matters most when children are also collecting. Ask SSA for the family maximum on the record before counting on full benefits for everyone.

Part C

When a husband dies

No one likes to plan for this part. But for most married women in a one-earner household, the survivor benefit is the largest Social Security decision of all, because it can last for decades.

13The survivor benefit: up to 100%

Frank claimed at 67 and Linda took her spouse's benefit at 67. Frank dies at 80.

The rule

A widow or widower at survivor full retirement age can get 100% of the deceased worker's benefit, including any delayed retirement credits. If she gets a benefit of her own that is smaller, only the difference is paid. You can't receive two benefits added together; SSA pays the higher one. The marriage must generally have lasted at least 9 months, with exceptions such as accidental death. (Handbook §407, §401, §404; SSA: What you could get from Survivor benefits)

Illustrative calculation
Household income before (Frank $2,800 + Linda $1,400)
$4,200
Linda as a widow (survivor benefit replaces her spouse's benefit)
$2,800
Change in household Social Security
−$1,400 (one-third)

Assumptions: base case; Linda is past survivor FRA when Frank dies.

Watch out

The household keeps the larger check, not both. Many widows find that housing and other costs don't fall by a third. That is why the size of the higher earner's benefit is really a joint decision.

14Died before claiming vs. after claiming early vs. after waiting to 70

Three versions of the same family. In each, Linda is at her survivor full retirement age when she claims.

The rule

The survivor rate is 100% of the worker's PIA plus delayed credits he earned or was eligible for at death. If the worker received a reduced (early) retirement benefit, the survivor benefit is reduced too. SSA's operations manual limits it to the larger of 82.5% of his PIA or the reduced amount he would be receiving if he were alive. (Handbook §407, §720; POMS RS 00615.320)

Illustrative calculation
Frank dies at 64, never claimed
$2,800
Frank claimed at 62 ($1,960), then died
$2,310 (82.5% of PIA)
Frank waited to 70 ($3,472), then died
$3,472

Assumptions: base case; Linda claims at survivor FRA (67); no own benefit; figures ignore cost-of-living adjustments and the recomputation SSA does at death.

Watch out

The gap between Frank claiming at 62 and at 70 is about $1,162 a month for Linda as a widow in this example, for the rest of her life. When the higher earner is in good health and the couple can bridge the gap, delaying is often described as life insurance for the survivor.

15Survivor benefits as early as 60, or 50 if disabled

Frank dies at 61. Linda is 60 and wonders whether to claim right away.

The rule

A widow or widower can claim at 60, or at 50 to 59 if disabled. Survivor payments start at 71.5% of the deceased's benefit and rise the longer you wait: over 75% at 61, over 80% at 63, over 90% at 65, and up to 100% at survivor full retirement age (66 to 67). SSA adds that you might be eligible regardless of age, for example if you are caring for a child of the person who died. (SSA: Who can get Survivor benefits; SSA: Survivor amounts; Handbook §724)

Illustrative calculation
Linda's survivor benefit at 60 (71.5%)
$2,002
at survivor FRA, 67 (100%)
$2,800

Assumptions: Frank never claimed, PIA $2,800; Linda has no own benefit and is not working.

Watch out

Before full retirement age, the earnings test applies to survivor benefits too. A widow who goes back to work at 60 may see benefits withheld. (SSA: Receiving benefits while working)

16Switching between a survivor benefit and her own

In this version, Linda had her own career with a PIA of $2,000. Frank dies at 64 without claiming. Linda is 60.

The rule

Deemed filing does not apply to survivor benefits. A widow can start one benefit and switch to the other later, for example taking a survivor benefit first and her own retirement benefit at 70, or the reverse. SSA's own example describes a 62-year-old widow who takes only the survivor benefit, lets her own grow, and switches at 70. (SSA: Filing rules; SSA: Survivor amounts)

Illustrative calculation
Path 1: survivor at 60 (71.5% × $2,800)
$2,002
…then her own at 70 (124% × $2,000)
$2,480
Path 2: her own at 62 (70% × $2,000)
$1,400
…then survivor at 67 (100% × $2,800)
$2,800

Assumptions: Linda's FRA and survivor FRA are 67; no earnings-test withholding. In Path 2, SSA pays her own benefit plus the difference up to the survivor amount.

Watch out

Which path is better depends on the two amounts and how long the widow expects to live. Ask SSA to show you both options before you sign, and be clear about which benefit you are applying for.

17The one-time $255 payment

After Frank's funeral, Linda's son mentions a death payment from Social Security.

The rule

SSA pays a one-time lump sum of $255, in addition to monthly benefits. It goes to a surviving spouse who was living in the same household, or, if there wasn't one, to a spouse or certain children eligible for benefits on the record. It is not payable to a divorced spouse, and you must apply within two years. (Handbook §428, §430, §431, §1517)

Illustrative calculation
Lump-sum death payment to Linda
$255 (one time)

Assumptions: Linda and Frank lived together when he died.

Watch out

The amount is small and fixed, but the call that requests it is also the moment to ask about the monthly survivor benefit, which is where the real money is. SSA notes the survivor application is not available online; it is taken by phone. (SSA: Fairness Act page, applying for survivor benefits)

18Remarrying before or after 60

Two years after Frank's death, Linda meets Tom. She asks whether marrying him would cost her the survivor benefit.

The rule

Remarriage at 60 or later (50 or later if disabled) does not stop survivor benefits on the earlier spouse's record. Remarriage before 60 generally means no survivor benefits on the earlier record unless that later marriage ends. After remarrying at 60 or later, she may also become eligible on her new husband's record, and SSA pays the larger amount. (Handbook §406, §409; SSA: Survivor eligibility; SSA blog, Aug. 22, 2024)

Illustrative calculation
Linda remarries at 62
Keeps survivor benefit
Linda remarries at 58
No survivor benefit while married to Tom

Assumptions: Linda is not disabled.

Watch out

The date of the wedding, not the date you apply, is what counts. If you are close to 60 and planning to remarry, check with SSA before setting the date.

Part D

Divorce and more than one marriage

19Ten years, unmarried, 62 or older

In this version, Frank and Linda divorced after 12 years of marriage. Linda never remarried. She is now 67.

The rule

A divorced spouse can collect on an ex-spouse's record if the marriage lasted 10 years or more before the divorce became final, she is not married, she is 62 or older, and her own benefit is less than half of his PIA. The benefit is figured the same way as a current spouse's: up to 50% of his PIA, reduced if she claims early. Unlike a current spouse, a divorced spouse cannot claim before 62 even with a child in care. (Handbook §311, §320; SSA: Family eligibility)

Illustrative calculation
Linda's divorced-spouse benefit at 67
$1,400
at 62
$910

Assumptions: base case; Frank has filed (or see Scenario 20); Linda has no own benefit.

Watch out

Nine years and eleven months is not ten years. If a divorce is in progress and the marriage is close to the 10-year mark, the date the divorce becomes final matters.

20Claiming before the ex files

Linda is 64. Frank, 63, is still working and has not applied. They have been divorced for five years.

The rule

A divorced spouse can be independently entitled even if the ex has not filed, as long as he is at least 62 and fully insured and they have been divorced for at least 2 continuous years. A divorced spouse also keeps getting benefits if the ex voluntarily suspends his own. (Handbook §311; SSA: Filing rules)

Illustrative calculation
Linda's divorced-spouse benefit at 64 (25% reduction)
$1,050
If they had divorced only one year earlier
$0 until Frank files

Assumptions: base case; Linda's FRA 67; Frank fully insured.

Watch out

Because she is divorced at least two years, Frank's paycheck also does not trigger earnings-test withholding from her benefit (Scenario 10).

21Two 10-year marriages: the higher one, not both

Linda was married to Frank for 11 years and later to Paul for 10 years. Both marriages ended in divorce. Paul's PIA is $3,400.

The rule

If you are eligible on more than one record, SSA pays the highest amount. Benefits are not added together. SSA's operations manual says an applicant eligible for reduced spouse's benefits on more than one record is deemed to have filed on all of them. (SSA: Family amounts; POMS GN 00204.035)

Illustrative calculation
On Frank's record (50% of $2,800)
$1,400
On Paul's record (50% of $3,400)
$1,700
What Linda receives at 67
$1,700

Assumptions: Linda unmarried, 67, no own benefit; both exes 62 or older.

Watch out

Tell SSA about every marriage that lasted 10 years, even if you think it won't matter. You can't choose the lower record to save the higher one for later.

22The ex remarried: both wives collect, and neither is cut

After the divorce, Frank married Joan. At 67, Frank files. Both Joan and Linda apply on his record.

The rule

A divorced spouse's benefit is not reduced by the family maximum, and her entitlement does not reduce benefits to the worker's current family. SSA's family benefits page says ex-spouses don't count toward the family maximum. (Handbook §320, §731; SSA: Family amounts)

Illustrative calculation
Frank
$2,800
Joan, current wife, at her FRA
$1,400

$1,400
Total on one record
$5,600

Assumptions: both wives at FRA, no own benefits, no children collecting.

Watch out

Claiming on an ex's record does not take anything from him or his new spouse. Many divorced women don't apply because they assume it would.

23She remarries

Linda, collecting as Frank's ex-wife, marries Bill at 68.

The rule

A divorced spouse must be unmarried to qualify. SSA says that generally, if you remarry, benefits paid to you on your former spouse's record stop, and you should report the marriage to avoid an overpayment. Because the requirement is being unmarried, if the later marriage ends, she can ask SSA about qualifying on Frank's record again. (Handbook §311; SSA blog, Aug. 22, 2024)

Illustrative calculation
Divorced-spouse benefit on Frank's record after marrying Bill
Generally stops
Spouse's benefit on Bill's record (after 1 year, if Bill has filed)
Up to 50% of Bill's PIA

Assumptions: Frank is alive. A widow's remarriage follows different rules (Scenario 18).

Watch out

Remarrying ends divorced-spouse benefits from a living ex at any age, unlike survivor benefits, which continue if you remarry at 60 or later.

24Surviving divorced spouse

Frank, Linda's ex-husband of 12 years, dies. Linda is 66 and single. Frank's widow, Joan, also applies.

The rule

An ex-spouse married at least 10 years may get survivor benefits on the same terms as a widow: from 60 (50 if disabled), up to 100% at survivor full retirement age, and continuing if she remarries at 60 or later. SSA says you might be eligible regardless of age or length of marriage in some cases, such as caring for the deceased's child. Benefits to a surviving divorced spouse do not count toward the family maximum, so Joan's benefit is not reduced. (SSA: Survivor eligibility; Survivor amounts; Handbook §731)

Watch out

The $255 lump sum is not payable to a divorced spouse (Scenario 17). Don't wait to be contacted: call SSA to apply.

Part E

Edge cases

26Same-sex marriage

Linda's friends Ann and Marie married in 2012 in a state that allowed it, but they lived in a state that did not recognize the marriage until later.

The rule

SSA recognizes a valid same-sex marriage as of the date of the marriage, including periods when the state of residence did not recognize it. Every rule in this guide applies the same way. For survivor benefits, if the marriage falls short of the nine-month requirement, SSA has procedures to consider whether an unconstitutional state law prevented the couple from marrying earlier. (POMS GN 00210.002)

Watch out

Civil unions, domestic partnerships and common-law marriages are handled under separate SSA rules and may need a legal review. If a claim was denied in the past because a marriage wasn't recognized, ask SSA whether it can be reopened.

27A pension from work outside Social Security

Linda's cousin, Rita, taught for 30 years in a school system that did not pay into Social Security. Her husband had a full career under Social Security. For years she was told her spouse's and survivor benefits would be cut or eliminated by her teacher's pension.

The rule

The Social Security Fairness Act, signed January 5, 2025, ended the Windfall Elimination Provision (WEP) and Government Pension Offset (GPO). The GPO had reduced or eliminated spouse's and surviving spouse's benefits for people with a pension from work not covered by Social Security. The change applies to benefits payable for January 2024 and later. People who never applied because of GPO may need to file now; retroactive benefits are generally limited to six months before the application. (SSA: Social Security Fairness Act)

Watch out

If you, a spouse or a parent skipped applying because of GPO, apply soon: the application date can affect when benefits begin. SSA warns that it will never charge to start or increase benefits, so ignore anyone who offers to “unlock” your increase for a fee.

28Taxes and Medicare

Frank and Linda file jointly. Their benefits, plus withdrawals from Frank's 401(k), put them in a higher tax bracket than they expected, and Linda is turning 65.

The rule

Taxes: up to 85% of Social Security benefits can be taxable. For a joint return, that starts when “combined income” (adjusted gross income, plus tax-exempt interest, plus half of benefits) exceeds $32,000; for an individual, $25,000. You can ask SSA to withhold federal tax at 7%, 10%, 12% or 22%. (SSA FAQ KA-02471; SSA: Withhold taxes)

Medicare: you sign up for Medicare Parts A and B through Social Security, and premiums can be withheld from benefit payments. SSA says spouses and survivors may be eligible for Medicare based on the worker's work history. (SSA: Sign up for Medicare; SSA: Family benefits; Survivor amounts)

Confirm your own numbers with SSA

  • Get your real figures. Each spouse can sign in to my Social Security to see benefit estimates and check the earnings record.
  • Gather documents. Marriage certificates, divorce decrees with final dates, and death certificates are what SSA asks about first.
  • Ask about every record. Current spouse, each 10-year ex, and any deceased spouse. SSA pays the highest, but only on records it knows about.
  • Call for survivor claims. SSA's number is 1-800-772-1213 (TTY 1-800-325-0778).

SSI vs. Social Security

Social Security retirement, spouse's and survivor benefits are earned through work and paid on a worker's record, regardless of your savings.

SSI (Supplemental Security Income) is a separate, needs-based program for people 65 or older, blind or disabled with little income and few resources. It isn't paid on a spouse's record.

This guide is about Social Security. Scenario 5 shows how the two interact.
