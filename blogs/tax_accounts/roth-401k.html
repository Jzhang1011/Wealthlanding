Priya's Catch-Up Had to Be Roth

Fictional example Priya Shah, 61, was ready to make a catch-up contribution the way she always had — pre-tax. For 2026, her wages from last year meant the catch-up itself had to be Roth.

Priya Shah is a fictional 61-year-old vice president whose 401(k) has offered a designated Roth account for years. She usually split deferrals by habit: pre-tax up to the ordinary limit, catch-up pre-tax too. For 2026 the law is more specific. The elective-deferral cap is shared between traditional and Roth. The age 60–63 super catch-up is $11,250. And if her FICA wages from this plan sponsor in the prior year exceeded $150,000, any catch-up contribution must be designated Roth.

A Roth 401(k) (or Roth 403(b), or designated Roth in a governmental 457(b)) is after-tax in, tax-free in growth, and tax-free on qualified withdrawals — the same broad design as a Roth IRA, but using the much larger workplace deferral limit, without a Roth IRA income phase-out. Employer matching contributions are still often made on a pre-tax basis, unless the plan lets her elect a Roth match (which is taxable when contributed). Beginning in 2024, designated Roth accounts in a workplace plan are no longer subject to lifetime RMDs. Traditional 401(k) balances still are. This story complements the traditional 401(k)/403(b) post and the 401(k) guide; it does not replace either.

For 2026, Priya’s combined traditional-plus-Roth elective deferrals cannot exceed $24,500, plus catch-up. At age 61 she is in the super-catch-up window: $11,250, for a combined elective ceiling of $35,750 if the plan allows it. The Roth catch-up wage threshold for 2026 is $150,000 of FICA wages from that sponsor in the prior year — an inflation-adjusted figure (do not use the older statutory $145,000 as if it were the 2026 test).

Worked examples and scenarios

Example 1

All Roth, including the super catch-up

Priya can elect the entire $35,750 as Roth in 2026 if the plan allows Roth deferrals and the super catch-up. There is no current-year federal income-tax deduction. Qualified withdrawals of that money, and of its earnings, can be tax-free, and the designated Roth account will not force lifetime RMDs. In a 32% federal bracket, choosing Roth instead of pre-tax means she forgoes a current-year deduction of $35,750 × 0.32 = $11,440 — the price of moving the tax into this year.

Example 2

The wage rule: catch-up must be Roth, base can still be pre-tax

Priya’s prior-year FICA wages from this employer were a fictional $165,000, above the $150,000 2026 threshold. Her catch-up must be Roth. Her $24,500 base deferral can still be traditional, Roth, or a split, according to the plan. One legal mix: $24,500 pre-tax plus $11,250 Roth catch-up. The pre-tax slice lowers 2026 taxable income by $24,500; in a 32% bracket that is $7,840 of federal income tax deferred this year. The $11,250 catch-up does not.

Example 3

RMDs: traditional versus designated Roth, since 2024

If Priya also holds a traditional 401(k) balance, that side remains subject to required minimum distributions at the statutory starting age. The designated Roth account does not, beginning with the 2024 change under SECURE 2.0. She can still roll a designated Roth 401(k) to a Roth IRA if she wants the IRA’s rules and investment menu. The point of this example is the split: two boxes inside one plan can now have different RMD fates.

Who it fits

  • Employees whose plans offer a designated Roth account and who expect similar or higher tax rates later — or who want to build a bucket without lifetime RMDs.
  • Workers ages 60–63 who can fund the $11,250 super catch-up.
  • High earners whose prior-year FICA wages exceed $150,000 (for 2026) and who therefore must make catch-ups as Roth anyway.

Who should skip — or wait

  • People in a very high current bracket who reasonably expect a much lower rate in retirement and who are not forced into a Roth catch-up — a traditional deferral may still be the better current-year trade, as the 401(k) guide explains.
  • Employees whose plan does not offer a designated Roth account (the wage rule then becomes a plan-design problem, not a personal election).
  • Anyone confusing “no RMDs on the Roth side” with “no tax on the traditional side.”

Common mistakes

  • Exceeding the shared $24,500 elective limit by funding traditional and Roth as if they were two caps.
  • Using $145,000 as the 2026 Roth catch-up wage test — the IRS figure for 2026 is $150,000.
  • Assuming employer match is automatically Roth, or assuming a Roth match is not taxable in the year it is contributed.
  • Forgetting that qualified-withdrawal rules (five years, and generally age 59½) still apply to designated Roth earnings.

Why this account matters

In 2026, catch-up contributions are larger at ages 60–63 — and, for higher-wage employees, they may have to be Roth. That is a planning fact, not a slogan about tax-free income.
