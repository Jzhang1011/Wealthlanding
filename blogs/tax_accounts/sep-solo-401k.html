Rob's Business Had No Employees. His Retirement Plan Did the Work.

Fictional example Rob Hale, 51, works for himself and has no employees. A SEP IRA and a solo 401(k) are both tax-advantaged. They are not the same size, and they are not tax-free on the way out if they are traditional accounts.

Rob Hale is a fictional 51-year-old consultant who files as a business owner and has no employees other than himself. Two plans show up in every “self-employed retirement” list: a SEP IRA and a solo (one-participant) 401(k). Both can be traditional, tax-deferred accounts: deductible or pre-tax contributions, tax-deferred growth, ordinary income tax on withdrawals, and RMDs later. A solo 401(k) can also offer designated Roth deferrals. A regular SEP is an employer-contribution plan; it does not have employee elective deferrals.

For 2026, the SEP contribution is the lesser of 25% of compensation or $72,000, using the IRS definition of compensation (and, for sole proprietors, net earnings after the deductible part of self-employment tax and after the contribution itself — which is why many sole-proprietor walkthroughs speak of a 20% factor). The same $72,000 is the annual-additions cap under section 415. A solo 401(k) can add elective deferrals of $24,500, plus an $8,000 catch-up at age 50 or older (or $11,250 at ages 60–63), on top of an employer profit-sharing contribution, so long as annual additions — deferrals plus employer, not counting the catch-up — stay within $72,000. With the age-50 catch-up, the ceiling becomes $80,000; at ages 60–63, $83,250. The compensation cap used in these formulas is $360,000 for 2026.

Deadlines differ, and they matter. A SEP can typically be established and funded up to the employer’s tax-filing deadline, including extensions. Elective deferrals to a solo 401(k) generally require the plan to have been established by year-end. A solo 401(k) may also have to file Form 5500-EZ once assets exceed the IRS threshold (commonly discussed as $250,000). If Rob later hires employees, both designs can stop being “solo” and start requiring coverage of those employees — this story assumes he remains a one-participant shop, possibly with a spouse.

Worked examples and scenarios

Example 1

A SEP only, on $200,000 of plan compensation

Suppose Rob operates as an S corporation and pays himself a reasonable salary of $200,000, which is also his SEP compensation in this illustration. Twenty-five percent of $200,000 is $50,000, under the $72,000 cap, so the 2026 SEP contribution can be $50,000. There is no additional employee elective deferral in a regular SEP, and no age-50 catch-up on top of that employer contribution. In a 32% federal bracket, $50,000 × 0.32 = $16,000 of federal income tax deferred this year.

Official 2026 SEP limit: lesser of 25% of compensation or $72,000. Salary is fictional. Sole-proprietor compensation uses a different net-earnings definition; do not copy the 25% factor onto net Schedule C profit without that adjustment.

Example 2

The same $200,000 salary in a solo 401(k), with catch-up

In a solo 401(k), Rob can elect $24,500 plus the $8,000 age-50 catch-up. An employer profit-sharing contribution of 25% of $200,000 would be $50,000, but elective deferrals and employer amounts together cannot exceed the $72,000 annual-additions cap (the $8,000 catch-up is in addition). So the employer piece is limited to $47,500 ($72,000 − $24,500). The 2026 total is $24,500 + $8,000 + $47,500 = $80,000 — $30,000 more than the SEP-only path on the same salary.

Example 3

A smaller sole-proprietor profit, where the deferral is the whole story

Now imagine Rob is a sole proprietor whose SEP compensation — net earnings after the deductible portion of self-employment tax and after the contribution — is a more modest $80,000. A 25% SEP contribution is $20,000. In a solo 401(k), he can still elect $24,500 plus $8,000 catch-up, and then add a smaller employer contribution up to the remaining room under the 415 cap and the 25% of compensation rule. Even if the employer piece were zero, the elective deferral plus catch-up alone ($32,500) already exceeds the SEP contribution. That is why, at moderate incomes, the solo 401(k) is often the larger traditional plan.

Who it fits

  • Self-employed people and owners with no employees (or only a spouse) who want a deductible, tax-deferred plan at 2026’s $72,000 / catch-up scale.
  • Owners with moderate compensation, for whom a solo 401(k)’s elective deferral and catch-up beat a SEP’s 25% employer contribution.
  • People who want the option of Roth elective deferrals inside a solo 401(k), if the plan document allows them.

Who should skip — or wait

  • Owners who already have a workplace 401(k) elsewhere and would exceed the shared elective-deferral cap across all plans.
  • Businesses with employees, until they have modeled coverage, testing, and cost — “solo” is not a label you can keep after you hire.
  • Anyone who needs the money in a few years; these are retirement plans with early-distribution rules, not tax-free checking accounts.

Common mistakes

  • Applying 25% to raw Schedule C profit without the sole-proprietor compensation adjustments.
  • Assuming a regular SEP has elective deferrals or an age-50 catch-up the way a 401(k) does.
  • Establishing a solo 401(k) too late to make a year-end elective deferral, or ignoring Form 5500-EZ once assets cross the filing threshold.
  • Exceeding the annual-additions cap by stacking a 25% employer contribution on top of a full deferral and catch-up without reducing the employer piece.

Why this account matters

For a one-person business, the plan choice is less about branding than about whether you can make an elective deferral and a catch-up. In 2026 those amounts are large enough to change the math.
