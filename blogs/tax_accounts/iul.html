Walter Was Pitched an IUL as a Tax-Free Retirement Plan

Fictional example Walter Greer, 59, was shown an indexed universal life policy as a way to take “tax-free” income in retirement. The tax rules are real. So are the caps, the fees, and the way a lapse can make a loan suddenly taxable.

Walter Greer is a fictional 59-year-old who sat through a presentation that described indexed universal life (IUL) as a tax-free retirement plan. IUL is a form of permanent life insurance. The cash value can be credited based on a market index, usually with a cap, a floor, a participation rate, and other moving parts the insurer can change on later premiums or segments. That is not the same thing as owning the index, and it is not a qualified retirement account.

The tax design, in outline, is the ordinary life-insurance design: cash value grows tax-deferred; a death benefit is generally income-tax-free to beneficiaries under IRC §101 if the usual conditions are met; and policy loans are generally not treated as taxable distributions if the policy is not a modified endowment contract (MEC) under IRC §7702A and if the policy stays in force. If Walter overfunds the policy too quickly, it can become a MEC, and loans or withdrawals are then taxed more like an annuity (gain first), often with an additional tax before 59½. If he takes large loans and the policy later lapses or is surrendered, the unpaid loan can help trigger taxable gain. Those are the conditions the “tax-free income” shorthand leaves out.

FINRA notes that many insurance products, including IUL, are generally not securities and are regulated as insurance, and that buyers should review caps, participation rates, fees, and surrender charges with care. This page is educational. It is not a product recommendation, and it does not use a carrier illustration. Any dollar figures below are labeled assumptions. The collection hub puts IUL last in the “max these first” order, after 401(k)/403(b), Roth IRA, HSA, and, if relevant, a Section 162 bonus that is at least honest about being taxable pay.

Costs matter as much as tax slogans. An IUL policy has cost of insurance charges that typically rise with age, plus expense charges, and often surrender charges in the early years. Illustrated “retirement income” depends on assumptions about future caps and participation rates that are not guarantees. NAIC buyer’s guides and state insurance regulators stress that illustrations are not promises. Walter’s first comparison is not one illustration against another. It is whether he has already used the 2026 qualified-plan room that actually is tax-advantaged by statute.

Worked examples and scenarios

Example 1

Opportunity cost against official 2026 qualified-plan limits

Walter is 59. If he still has workplace-plan room, 2026 elective deferrals are $24,500 plus an $8,000 catch-up, or $11,250 if he were 60–63. An IRA or Roth IRA can take another $8,600 if he is eligible. An HSA, if he has a qualifying HDHP and is not on Medicare, can take $4,400 or $8,750 plus a $1,000 catch-up at 55+. Those accounts have statutory contribution limits, published IRS rules, and — in the Roth and HSA cases — a clearer path to tax-free qualified withdrawals. Premium dollars that go into an IUL instead of those accounts are an opportunity-cost decision, not a free upgrade.

Example 2

A lapse with a loan can turn “tax-free” into taxable gain

This example is a labeled hypothetical, not a policy illustration. Suppose Walter has paid $50,000 of premiums (his basis), the cash value later shows $80,000, and he has an outstanding policy loan of $70,000. If the policy stays in force and is not a MEC, the loan itself is generally not a taxable distribution. If the policy lapses or is surrendered with that loan outstanding, the tax calculation can treat the unpaid loan as part of the amount realized. Gain is roughly the amount realized minus basis; in a simplified telling, a lapse could produce taxable income on the order of $30,000 ($80,000 minus $50,000) — and the loan does not simply vanish tax-free. The exact figure depends on IRS life-insurance contract tax rules and the insurer’s reporting. The educational point is the condition: tax-free loans require a policy that stays in force.

Example 3

MEC versus non-MEC treatment of a withdrawal

If Walter funds the policy slowly enough to stay outside IRC §7702A, a withdrawal is generally treated as basis first, and a loan can be tax-free while the contract stays in force. If the same policy is a modified endowment contract — typically because it was funded too quickly under the seven-pay test — a withdrawal or loan is generally taxed as gain first, and an additional 10% tax can apply before age 59½, with exceptions. Two policies with the same cash value can therefore have very different tax results on the same $10,000 withdrawal. That is a statutory distinction, not a marketing comparison.

Who it fits

  • People who have a durable need for permanent life insurance, can fund a policy for many years, and have already maxed (or cannot use) 401(k), IRA, and HSA room.
  • Buyers who will read the cap, participation rate, floor, loan interest, cost of insurance, and surrender-charge schedule, and who understand that illustrations are not guarantees.
  • Households that can keep the policy in force so that a loan strategy does not collapse into a taxable lapse.

Who should skip — or wait

  • Anyone who has not yet used available 401(k), 403(b), IRA, Roth IRA, or HSA room. Those accounts come first.
  • People buying an IUL primarily as an “investment” or a “tax-free retirement plan,” especially if they do not need the death benefit.
  • Anyone who will need flexible access to cash and cannot tolerate rising insurance charges, a surrender period, or the risk that caps and participation rates change.

Common mistakes

  • Treating an illustration’s retirement-income column as a guarantee, or ignoring that caps, participation rates, and charges can change.
  • Overfunding into MEC status and then taking loans as if they were still tax-free.
  • Borrowing aggressively and letting the policy lapse, which can create a tax bill in a year you may not have cash.
  • Paying premiums instead of a workplace match, a 2026 catch-up contribution, or an HSA.
