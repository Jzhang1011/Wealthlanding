Nina Kept Waiting for the Perfect Year to Open a Roth IRA

Fictional example Nina Alvarez, 47, had earned income every year and still treated a Roth IRA as something she would start “when the timing was right.” The 2026 limits and phase-outs are already on the books.

Nina Alvarez is a fictional 47-year-old project lead with W-2 wages. She kept waiting for a year when her income would be lower, or her budget looser, to open a Roth IRA. A Roth IRA does not require a special year. It requires earned income (or a spousal IRA), a contribution within the annual limit, and, for a direct contribution, modified AGI inside the official phase-out range.

The tax design is the opposite of a traditional 401(k): contributions are not deductible, growth is not taxed along the way, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. Contributions themselves can generally be withdrawn at any time, in any amount, without tax or penalty; earnings have a five-year clock and, in most cases, an age-59½ rule before they are qualified. Original owners of Roth IRAs are not subject to lifetime required minimum distributions.

Our Roth IRA guide remains the indexed reference for how a Roth sits in the tax pyramid. This story works the 2026 numbers — including the phase-out arithmetic — for readers in the 45–65 years. If income is already above the direct-contribution range, see the backdoor Roth guide rather than a second “Roth IRA guide.”

For 2026, the IRA contribution limit is $7,500, or $8,600 if you are age 50 or older (a $1,100 catch-up). The 2026 Roth IRA MAGI phase-out ranges are $153,000–$168,000 for single filers and heads of household, $242,000–$252,000 for married filing jointly, and $0–$10,000 for married filing separately (if you lived with your spouse). Those ranges come from the same IRS COLA announcement that raised the contribution limit.

Worked examples and scenarios

Example 1

Nina, 47, is under the phase-out and can contribute the full $7,500

Nina files single. Her 2026 MAGI is a fictional $140,000 — below the $153,000 starting point of the single phase-out. If she has at least $7,500 of earned income, she can contribute the full $7,500 to a Roth IRA. There is no current-year federal deduction. The trade is that qualified withdrawals later can be tax-free.

Example 2

A partial contribution inside the single phase-out

If Nina’s MAGI were instead a fictional $160,000, she would be $7,000 into the $15,000 single phase-out range ($153,000 to $168,000). The 2026 limit is reduced in proportion: $7,500 × ($168,000 − $160,000) / $15,000 = $4,000. She could contribute $4,000 directly to a Roth IRA, not $7,500. At $168,000 or above, the direct Roth IRA limit is zero.

Example 3

The catch-up year, and a workplace plan in the same year

When Nina turns 50, the IRA catch-up adds $1,100, for a 2026-style limit of $8,600 (future years may be different). That IRA limit is separate from her workplace elective deferral. She can fund a Roth IRA and a 401(k) in the same year if she has the cash flow and meets each account’s rules. The Roth IRA does not have lifetime RMDs for the original owner; a traditional 401(k) does.

Who it fits

  • Anyone with earned income (or a spouse with earned income, for a spousal IRA) who wants tax-free qualified withdrawals later.
  • People who expect the same or higher tax rates in retirement, or who want an account without lifetime RMDs.
  • Savers who have already captured a workplace match and still have room in the household budget.

Who should skip — or wait

  • Households with no earned income and no spousal-IRA path.
  • Filers over the MAGI phase-out who have not considered the backdoor Roth path — or who have large pre-tax IRA balances that make the pro-rata rule painful. See the backdoor Roth guide.
  • Anyone who needs the money for a near-term non-qualified purpose and is counting on the earnings being tax-free before the five-year and age tests are met.

Common mistakes

  • Contributing more than earned income, or exceeding the $7,500 / $8,600 cap across all of your traditional and Roth IRAs combined.
  • Assuming a direct Roth contribution is allowed because a coworker of the same age can contribute — the phase-out is based on MAGI and filing status.
  • Confusing a Roth IRA with a traditional 401(k): one is after-tax in and potentially tax-free out; the other is pre-tax in and taxed out.
  • Missing the five-year clock on earnings, or on a conversion, when planning an early withdrawal.
