James Thought a Coverdell Was Just a Smaller 529

Fictional example James Ortiz, 49, liked the idea of a Coverdell ESA because it sounded like a 529 with more investment flexibility. The $2,000 cap and the income phase-out are what actually define it.

James Ortiz is a fictional 49-year-old parent who almost opened a Coverdell education savings account because a friend described it as “a 529 you can invest like a brokerage account.” Some of that is directionally true: a Coverdell ESA is funded with after-tax dollars, can grow tax-free, and can be withdrawn tax-free for qualified education expenses, including a broad set of elementary and secondary costs. What the shorthand leaves out is the tight contribution cap, the contributor’s income phase-out, and the age rules.

IRS Topic 310 is the short official summary: the most that can be contributed for a beneficiary is $2,000 in a year, across all Coverdell ESAs for that beneficiary. Contributions generally must stop when the beneficiary turns 18 (special-needs exceptions exist), and unused amounts generally must be distributed within 30 days after the beneficiary turns 30 (again with a special-needs exception). A 529 has no federal annual contribution cap of this kind, no federal contributor income phase-out, and no comparable age-18 contribution cutoff. For most families saving more than $2,000 a year, the 529 is the larger tool; the 529 story and the 529 guide are the place to start.

ContributionsAfter-taxNo federal deduction. Contributions are limited to $2,000 per beneficiary per year and can be phased out by MAGI.
GrowthTax-free if used qualifiedEarnings are not taxed while they stay in the ESA.
WithdrawalsTax-free if qualifiedQualified elementary, secondary, and higher-education expenses can come out tax-free. Non-qualified earnings: income tax plus a 10% additional tax, with exceptions.

The MAGI phase-out for contributors is set in IRC §530 and described in IRS Publication 970. In Publication 970 for 2025, the phase-out is $95,000–$110,000 for single filers and $190,000–$220,000 for married filing jointly. Those figures have long followed the statute and are not routinely increased the way 401(k) and IRA limits are. A 2026 Publication 970 was not in hand when this page was written, so the ranges below are labeled as Publication 970 (2025) / IRC §530 figures. If a later official publication changes them, use that publication.

Worked examples and scenarios

Example 1

The $2,000 cap is a beneficiary cap

James wants to contribute, and so does the child’s grandparent. The cap is $2,000 per beneficiary per year, not $2,000 per contributor. If James contributes $1,500 and a grandparent contributes $800, $300 is excess and has to be corrected under the Coverdell excess-contribution rules. The same $2,000 would barely buy a month of some private-school tuitions; a 529 is built for larger amounts.

Example 2

A married couple inside the phase-out

James and his spouse file jointly. Their MAGI in this illustration is $200,000. Using the Publication 970 (2025) / IRC §530 joint phase-out of $190,000–$220,000, they are $10,000 into a $30,000 range. The $2,000 limit is reduced by $2,000 × $10,000 / $30,000 = $667 (rounded), leaving about $1,333 of contribution room. At $220,000 or more of MAGI, the direct Coverdell limit is zero. A 529 contribution would not face this federal income test.

Example 3

When a Coverdell can still have a role beside a 529

The child’s qualified K-12 expenses include items a Coverdell has historically covered broadly (such as certain books, supplies, and, where the rules allow, technology used for school). After December 31, 2025, a 529 can also cover a wider set of elementary and secondary expenses, up to $20,000 a year. Even then, a household that wants a particular investment menu inside an ESA, and that can live with $2,000 a year and the age-30 distribution rule, might use both — without paying the same expense from both accounts and also claiming an education credit on that same dollar.

Who it fits

  • Contributors whose MAGI is under the phase-out and who want a small, education-specific account with broad K-12 qualified expenses.
  • Families already using a 529 who have a modest extra $2,000 and a reason to use an ESA’s investment or expense rules.
  • People saving for a beneficiary who is still under the age-18 contribution cutoff (or who qualifies for a special-needs exception).

Who should skip — or wait

  • Contributors over the MAGI phase-out — a 529 has no federal income cap on who may contribute.
  • Anyone trying to save more than $2,000 a year for education; the 529 is the scalable account.
  • Households that will still need the money in the account after the beneficiary turns 30, unless a special-needs exception or a timely beneficiary change applies.

Common mistakes

  • Treating $2,000 as a per-contributor limit, or funding a Coverdell after the beneficiary turns 18 without an exception.
  • Ignoring the contributor income phase-out, which a 529 does not have at the federal level.
  • Leaving the account in place past the age-30 distribution rule and triggering tax and the additional tax on earnings.
  • Double-counting the same education expense with a 529, a Coverdell, and an education credit.
