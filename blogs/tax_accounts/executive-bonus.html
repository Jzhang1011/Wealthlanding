Andrea's Bonus Came With a Life Insurance Policy

Fictional example Andrea Feldman, 54, was offered an “executive bonus plan” that would pay premiums on a life insurance policy in her name. The tax mechanics are simpler than the nickname: it is a bonus, and bonuses are taxed as pay.

Andrea Feldman is a fictional 54-year-old executive. Her employer offered what the benefits memo called an executive bonus plan, sometimes described as a Section 162 plan: the company would bonus her enough money to pay premiums on a permanent life insurance policy that she would own. That structure can be a legitimate form of additional compensation plus life insurance. It is not a 401(k), it is not tax-free income, and it should not jump the line ahead of qualified accounts.

Under a classic design, the employer pays a bonus. The bonus is taxable wages to Andrea (income tax, and generally payroll tax). The employer deducts the bonus as ordinary and necessary compensation under IRC §162, if the amount is reasonable. Andrea owns the policy; the death benefit, if the policy stays in force, is generally income-tax-free to her beneficiaries under the usual life-insurance rules. A restrictive endorsement may limit her access to cash value for a period, which is why these plans sometimes feel like golden handcuffs. A double bonus is a larger paycheck: the employer bonuses enough to cover both the premium and the tax on the bonus, so Andrea is not writing a personal check for the tax. None of this is a product recommendation. It is a tax-and-mechanics walkthrough, with a clear “max these first” order. See the collection hub for that order.

Before the arithmetic, the ordering rule: Andrea should already be on track to capture her workplace match, fund an HSA if she is eligible, and use her 2026 401(k) elective deferral and catch-up ($24,500 plus $8,000 at age 50+, or $11,250 at ages 60–63). A Roth IRA or backdoor Roth, if relevant, still belongs ahead of an insurance-funded bonus arrangement. An executive bonus plan can then add life insurance and additional compensation. It should not be sold as a substitute for those accounts.

Worked examples and scenarios

Example 1

A single bonus that covers only the premium

The policy premium in this illustration is $20,000 a year. That figure is an assumption, not a quote or an illustration from an insurer. If the employer bonuses exactly $20,000 and Andrea’s combined marginal rate on the bonus — labeled here as an illustrative 35% of federal income tax, state tax, and payroll tax combined — is 35%, she owes $7,000 of tax on the bonus and nets $13,000 toward the premium. She must find another $7,000 from already-taxed money if she wants the premium paid in full.

Example 2

A double bonus, so the premium is covered after tax

To leave $20,000 after tax at the same illustrative 35% combined rate, the employer must bonus $20,000 / (1 − 0.35) = $30,769 (rounded). Tax on that bonus is $30,769 × 0.35 = $10,769. After tax, $20,000 remains for the premium. If the $30,769 is reasonable compensation, the employer deducts $30,769 — not $20,000 — as a Section 162 compensation expense in this telling. Whether a deduction is allowed in fact depends on the employer’s circumstances, reasonableness, and other tax rules.

Example 3

Opportunity cost against a 2026 401(k) catch-up year

Andrea is 54. If she has not yet deferred the 2026 401(k) maximum, $24,500 plus the $8,000 catch-up is $32,500 of tax-deferred (or Roth) workplace savings, plus any match, inside a qualified plan with statutory contribution limits and ERISA or other plan protections. A $20,000 or $30,769 insurance bonus does not use that cap and does not replace it. If cash flow can only fund one, the qualified plan is the first account. If cash flow can fund both, the bonus can be evaluated as compensation-plus-insurance — including any restrictive endorsement that limits access to cash value if she leaves.

Who it fits

  • Executives and key employees whose employer wants to provide additional compensation and a life insurance benefit, and who already max (or are on track to max) qualified plans.
  • Employees who need permanent life insurance and prefer to own the policy, even if a restrictive endorsement delays access to cash value.
  • Employers looking for a deductible compensation expense rather than a more complex split-dollar or nonqualified deferred-compensation design — after legal and tax review.

Who should skip — or wait

  • Anyone who has not yet funded a workplace match, 401(k)/403(b) deferral and catch-up, HSA, or IRA. Those come first.
  • People who do not need (or cannot qualify for) life insurance and are being shown the plan primarily as a “tax-free retirement” vehicle.
  • Employees who may leave soon and would be stuck with a restrictive endorsement, a surrender charge, or a policy they no longer want to fund.

Common mistakes

  • Hearing “Section 162 plan” and assuming the bonus is tax-free to the employee. It is taxed as wages.
  • Skipping the 401(k), HSA, or IRA in order to pay a premium, or treating a policy illustration as a guaranteed retirement paycheck.
  • Ignoring payroll tax, state tax, and the cost of a double-bonus gross-up for the employer.
  • Failing to read the restrictive endorsement, vesting, and what happens to the policy if employment ends.

Why this account matters

An executive bonus plan is additional taxable pay used to buy a policy the employee owns. That can be a rational benefit. It is a poor substitute for a 401(k), an HSA, or an IRA.
