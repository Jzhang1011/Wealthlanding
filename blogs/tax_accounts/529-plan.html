Carol Opened a 529 for a Grandchild She Hadn't Met Yet

Fictional example Carol Whitaker, 58, opened a 529 in a grandchild’s name before the child was born. The tax design is straightforward. The newer withdrawal and rollover rules are easier to miss.

Carol Whitaker is a fictional 58-year-old grandparent. She opened a 529 plan account, naming a grandchild as beneficiary, because she wanted a defined place for education gifts. A 529 (a qualified tuition program under IRC §529) is funded with after-tax dollars. There is no federal deduction. Growth is tax-deferred and can be withdrawn tax-free for qualified education expenses. That is a tax-advantaged education account — not a retirement account, and not a place to stash money ahead of her own 401(k) or HSA.

Our 529 guide remains the indexed reference for where a 529 sits in the tax pyramid and why retirement saving comes first. This story updates the withdrawal rules that have changed and works them through Carol’s case: the K-12 cap is now $20,000 a year for distributions after December 31, 2025; qualified uses have expanded; and unused money may, in tightly limited cases, move to the beneficiary’s Roth IRA. State tax deductions or credits, if any, depend on the state and often on using that state’s plan.

ContributionsAfter-tax (federal)No federal deduction. Some states offer a deduction or credit, often only for their own plan.
GrowthTax-free if used qualifiedEarnings are not taxed federally while they stay in the plan.
WithdrawalsTax-free if qualifiedQualified education withdrawals are tax-free. Non-qualified withdrawals: income tax plus a 10% additional tax on the earnings, with exceptions.

IRS Topic 313 (reviewed November 9, 2026) is the primary short-form source for current qualified expenses. For distributions after December 31, 2025, up to $20,000 per year from all of a beneficiary’s qualified tuition programs can be used for certain elementary and secondary (K-12) expenses; the cap was $10,000 for earlier distributions. Qualified uses also include certain curriculum and instructional materials, tutoring, testing fees, apprenticeship expenses, a limited amount of student-loan repayment, and recognized postsecondary credentialing expenses — always as the Code and IRS describe them, not as a plan brochure summarizes them. The 529-to-Roth IRA rollover, added by SECURE 2.0, is a trustee-to-trustee transfer, counts against the beneficiary’s annual Roth IRA contribution limit, is capped at $35,000 in a lifetime, requires the 529 to have been open for more than 15 years, and can include only contributions (and earnings on those contributions) made before the five-year period ending on the date of the distribution.

Worked examples and scenarios

Example 1

A qualified college withdrawal, in dollars

Carol’s 529, in this fictional telling, later holds $40,000, of which $28,000 is contribution basis and $12,000 is earnings. The grandchild’s eligible college bills $18,000 of qualified expenses in a year. An $18,000 qualified withdrawal is federal-tax-free — including the earnings portion. If Carol instead withdrew $18,000 for a non-qualified purpose, the earnings slice of that withdrawal would be taxable and, unless an exception applied, subject to a 10% additional tax.

Qualified college bill$18,000
Federal tax if qualified$0

Account values are fictional and labeled. Tax treatment follows IRS Topic 313 and Publication 970. State tax and plan fees are separate.

Example 2

K-12 after December 31, 2025: $20,000, not $10,000

The grandchild later attends a private elementary school with $22,000 of tuition. For a distribution after December 31, 2025, up to $20,000 from all of that beneficiary’s 529s can be treated as a qualified K-12 expense for the year (the cap was $10,000 before that date). The remaining $2,000 of tuition in this example is not covered by that cap; paying it from the 529 would be a non-qualified distribution of whatever portion is allocated to earnings, unless another qualified-expense category applies. The $20,000 figure is a beneficiary-level annual cap, not a per-account cap.

K-12 cap after Dec. 31, 2025$20,000
Prior cap$10,000
Tuition above the cap in this example$2,000

Official rule from IRS Topic 313, reviewed November 9, 2026. Tuition amount is fictional.

Example 3

A 529-to-Roth rollover is narrow on purpose

Fifteen years after Carol opened the account, unused money remains. The beneficiary, now working and eligible to contribute to a Roth IRA, may receive a trustee-to-trustee rollover from the 529 — but only within the beneficiary’s annual Roth IRA limit ($7,500 in 2026, or $8,600 if age 50 or older), only up to a $35,000 lifetime cap, and only from contributions (and their earnings) that were in the account before the five-year period ending on the distribution date. A $7,500 rollover in 2026 would use up that year’s Roth IRA contribution room. It would not be a way to move $35,000 in a single year.

Lifetime 529-to-Roth cap$35,000
2026 annual Roth IRA limit$7,500 ($8,600 at 50+)
Account-age testOpen more than 15 years

IRS Publication 590-A and Topic 313. The beneficiary must have earned income for the Roth IRA contribution, as usual.

Who it fits

  • Parents or grandparents who have already funded their own retirement basics (workplace match, HSA if eligible, IRA or 401(k)) and want a defined education account.
  • Families who expect qualified higher-education, K-12, apprenticeship, or credentialing costs, and who can live with the qualified-expense rules.
  • People who want the option — years later, if the rules still hold — to move a limited amount of unused 529 money to the beneficiary’s Roth IRA.

Who should skip — or wait

  • Anyone funding a 529 before a workplace match, HSA, or urgent high-interest debt is handled. Retirement still comes first; the 529 guide says the same.
  • Savers who need the money for non-education goals in the next few years and would likely owe tax and a 10% additional tax on earnings.
  • Households that would be better served, for a modest K-12 amount, by comparing a Coverdell ESA’s different limits and investment rules — see the Coverdell story.

Common mistakes

  • Using the old $10,000 K-12 cap for distributions after December 31, 2025, or treating $20,000 as a per-account cap rather than a per-beneficiary cap.
  • Assuming any leftover 529 can be dumped into a Roth IRA in one year. The annual Roth limit, the $35,000 lifetime cap, the 15-year clock, and the five-year seasoning rule all apply.
  • Paying the same expense from a 529 and also claiming an education credit for that same dollar.
  • Ignoring state-plan tax benefits, fees, and investment menus — or assuming a federal deduction that does not exist.

Why this account matters
