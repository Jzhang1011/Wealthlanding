Mark's HSA Clock Started Ticking at 55

Fictional example Mark Chen, 55, finally had a high-deductible health plan that qualified. The HSA catch-up had opened. So had a quieter deadline: once Medicare enrollment starts, HSA contributions have to stop.

Mark Chen is a fictional 55-year-old on a family high-deductible health plan (HDHP). An HSA is available only if he is covered by a qualifying HDHP, is not enrolled in disqualifying other coverage, and — this matters in the decade before Medicare — is not enrolled in Medicare. When those tests are met, the tax design is unusually strong: contributions can be pre-tax (or deductible), growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free.

That “triple” treatment is why this account is tax-advantaged in a deeper way than a traditional 401(k), and why it is easy to oversell as magic. It still depends on HDHP eligibility, on keeping receipts, and on not treating the account as a regular checking account. Our HSA guide remains the indexed reference. This story works the 2026 limits and the 55–65 timing issues, including the rule that Medicare enrollment stops contributions. Compare plan designs in the health-plan comparison tool, and see the collection hub for how an HSA sits next to an FSA.

ContributionsPre-tax or deductiblePayroll contributions often reduce income tax and FICA. Direct contributions may be deductible. The annual cap applies.
GrowthTax-freeInterest, dividends, and gains are not taxed while the money stays in the HSA.
WithdrawalsTax-free if qualifiedQualified medical expenses are tax-free. Non-qualified withdrawals are taxable, plus an additional 20% tax before age 65.

Revenue Procedure 2025-19 set the 2026 HSA contribution limits at $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage. The statutory catch-up for people age 55 or older remains $1,000. For 2026, a qualifying HDHP must have a minimum deductible of $1,700 (self-only) or $3,400 (family), and a maximum out-of-pocket of $8,500 or $17,000. Enrolling in Medicare — including Part A — ends HSA eligibility; the Form 8889 instructions are the usual IRS pointer for that stop date.

Worked examples and scenarios

Example 1

Mark, 55, with family HDHP coverage

In 2026, the family limit is $8,750. Because Mark is 55 or older, he can add the $1,000 catch-up, for a combined ceiling of $9,750 if he stays HSA-eligible all year. In a 24% federal income-tax bracket, $9,750 × 0.24 = $2,340 of federal income tax deferred or avoided this year. If the contribution is made by payroll reduction, FICA tax of 7.65% on $9,750 is another $746 (rounded).

Example 2

The Medicare stop date

Suppose Mark plans to enroll in Medicare at 65 in mid-year. HSA contributions must stop once he is enrolled in Medicare. A full-year contribution is not available for a year that includes months of Medicare enrollment; the limit is prorated for the months he is eligible. Delaying Medicare Part A to keep contributing can have other consequences — including for Social Security, if he is also claiming benefits — so this is a coordination question, not a loophole. The arithmetic is simple only if he stays eligible for all 12 months.

Example 3

A non-qualified withdrawal after 65, versus a qualified one

If Mark later uses HSA money for qualified medical expenses — including many Medicare premiums, though not Medigap — the withdrawal can remain tax-free, even decades after the expense if he keeps records. If he instead takes a non-qualified withdrawal after age 65, the additional 20% tax no longer applies, but the amount is still ordinary income. A $5,000 non-qualified withdrawal in a 22% federal bracket would cost $1,100 of federal income tax. The same $5,000 used for a qualified medical expense would cost $0 in tax.

Who it fits

  • People with a qualifying HDHP who can fund the deductible from cash flow or from the HSA itself.
  • Workers 55–64 who can use the $1,000 catch-up and are not yet enrolled in Medicare.
  • Households that can leave the HSA invested for future medical costs, including costs in retirement.

Who should skip — or wait

  • Anyone without a qualifying HDHP, or with disqualifying coverage (including Medicare enrollment, or a general-purpose health FSA).
  • Households that would struggle to meet the HDHP deductible and would be better served by a different medical plan — run the comparison first.
  • People a few months from Medicare enrollment who assume they can still make a full-year contribution.

Common mistakes

  • Pairing a general-purpose health FSA with an HSA — that usually ends HSA eligibility. A limited-purpose (dental/vision) FSA is the usual workplace workaround; see the FSA story.
  • Losing receipts and later treating qualified medical spending as if it were undocumented.
  • Enrolling in Medicare (even Part A automatically with Social Security) and continuing to contribute.
  • Using the HSA as a spending account for non-medical costs before 65 and paying both income tax and the 20% additional tax.

Why this account matters

Between 55 and Medicare, the HSA catch-up is open and the eligibility clock is running. That combination is easy to miss if the account is treated as just another workplace perk.
