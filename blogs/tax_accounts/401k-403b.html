Diane Still Thought of It as the Company Plan

Fictional example Diane Okoye, 52, still called it “the company plan.” The 2026 numbers — and the difference between a tax break now and a tax-free withdrawal later — were more precise than the nickname.

Diane Okoye, a fictional 52-year-old operations manager, thought of her 401(k) the way many people do: money the company helped her put away, to be dealt with later. That shorthand hides the tax design. A traditional 401(k) or 403(b) reduces taxable income when she contributes, grows tax-deferred, and is taxed as ordinary income when she withdraws. That is a tax-advantaged account. It is not a tax-free account.

A 403(b) — common at public schools, hospitals, and other 501(c)(3) employers — uses the same 2026 elective-deferral and catch-up limits as a 401(k). Governmental 457(b) plans and the Thrift Savings Plan share the $24,500 elective limit as well. The plan document still decides the menu, the match, and the vesting schedule.

This story sits beside our reference traditional 401(k) guide. Use the guide for the tax-pyramid framing; use the examples below for 2026 catch-up arithmetic in the years from 45 to 65. The tax-advantaged accounts hub maps how this plan sits next to a Roth IRA, a Roth 401(k), and an HSA.

ContributionsPre-taxElective deferrals usually lower federal taxable wages. They generally do not reduce Social Security or Medicare wages.
GrowthTax-deferredDividends, interest, and gains are not taxed while the money stays in the plan.
WithdrawalsTaxed as incomeOrdinary income tax applies. A 10% additional tax can apply before 59½, with exceptions. Required minimum distributions apply to traditional balances.

For 2026, the IRS set the elective-deferral limit at $24,500. The age-50 catch-up is $8,000, so Diane can defer $32,500 if the plan allows catch-ups. From ages 60 through 63, SECURE 2.0’s “super” catch-up is $11,250 in 2026 — a total elective deferral of $35,750. The annual-additions (section 415) cap is $72,000, or $80,000 with the age-50 catch-up, or $83,250 at ages 60–63. The compensation cap used in several plan tests is $360,000.

Beginning in 2026, if Diane’s FICA wages from this plan sponsor in the prior year exceeded $150,000, any catch-up she makes must be designated Roth — if the plan offers catch-up contributions at all. That rule does not turn her regular $24,500 deferral into Roth money; it only governs the catch-up slice. Our Roth 401(k) story walks through that case.

Worked examples and scenarios

Example 1

Diane, 52, fills the 2026 deferral and catch-up

If her plan allows catch-ups, Diane can elect $24,500 plus the $8,000 age-50 catch-up, for $32,500. In a 24% federal income-tax bracket, that deferral reduces this year’s federal income tax by $7,800 ($32,500 × 0.24). State tax, if any, would add to the current-year savings. The same dollars will be taxed later as ordinary income when she withdraws them — the rate then may be higher or lower than 24%.

Example 2

A colleague at 62 uses the super catch-up

In 2026, a participant who is age 60, 61, 62, or 63 can use the $11,250 super catch-up instead of the $8,000 amount, for a combined elective deferral of $35,750 if the plan allows it. In a 32% federal bracket, $35,750 × 0.32 = $11,440 of federal income tax deferred this year. At 64, the super catch-up no longer applies; the ordinary age-50 catch-up of $8,000 does.

Example 3

The workplace plan and a Roth IRA in the same year

The 401(k) limit is separate from the IRA limit. If Diane also has earned income and her income allows a direct Roth IRA contribution, she can still add $8,600 to a Roth IRA in 2026 ($7,500 plus the $1,100 IRA catch-up at 50+). That account is a different tax design: after-tax in, tax-free qualified withdrawals. If her income is too high for a direct Roth IRA, the backdoor Roth guide explains the alternative path.

Two separate official 2026 caps. Employer matching contributions, if any, are extra and count toward the annual-additions limit — not toward the elective-deferral cap.

Who it fits

  • Employees whose employer offers a 401(k) or 403(b), especially once the match is on the table.
  • People in a high current tax bracket who value a deduction today and can live with ordinary-income tax on withdrawals and future RMDs.
  • Workers ages 50–65 who can use the $8,000 catch-up — or the $11,250 amount at 60–63 — while they still have earned wages.

Who should skip — or wait

  • Anyone without a workplace plan — look instead at a SEP IRA or solo 401(k) if you are self-employed, or a Roth IRA if you have earned income.
  • Households that expect to be in a meaningfully higher tax bracket in retirement may prefer Roth deferrals for some or all of the same limit.
  • High earners subject to the 2026 Roth catch-up wage rule who assume a pre-tax catch-up is still available — check the plan and last year’s FICA wages first.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving the match on the table, or cashing out a balance when changing jobs and turning tax-deferred growth into a taxable distribution.
  • Treating the account as “tax-free” because the paycheck looks smaller. Withdrawals of traditional balances are taxable.
  • Ignoring the 2026 Roth catch-up rule, or assuming the $11,250 super catch-up applies after age 63.
  • Forgetting that elective deferrals and employer contributions share the annual-additions cap.

Why this account matters

For many employees between 45 and 65, the workplace plan is still the largest tax-advantaged bucket they will ever fill — especially once catch-up contributions open. Knowing that it is tax-deferred, not tax-free, changes how it sits next to a Roth IRA or Roth 401(k).
