Elena's Three Envelopes

Fictional example Elena Brooks, 46, had three workplace “spending accounts” on her benefits sheet. Only one could sit next to an HSA. None of them was tax-free in the Roth sense.

Elena Brooks is a fictional 46-year-old employee with a school-age child and a benefits portal that listed three similar-looking envelopes: a health flexible spending arrangement, a limited-purpose FSA, and a dependent care FSA. All three can be funded with pre-tax dollars through a cafeteria plan. None of them is a retirement account, and none of them is “tax-free” the way a qualified Roth withdrawal is. They are use-it-carefully accounts for defined expenses, usually inside a single plan year.

A general-purpose health FSA pays qualified medical expenses. A limited-purpose FSA is typically restricted to dental and vision care so that it can coexist with an HSA — a general-purpose health FSA usually makes someone ineligible to contribute to an HSA. A dependent care FSA pays qualifying child or dependent care so that Elena (and her spouse, if she is married) can work. The 18–25 FSA note on this site is a hidden, noindex page and is not the public guide; this story is the public, indexed explanation, and it links back to the collection hub and the HSA story.

ContributionsPre-taxPayroll reductions usually lower federal income tax and, in a cafeteria plan, FICA wages. There is no Roth-style after-tax option.
GrowthNot an investment accountAn FSA is a spending arrangement for a plan year, not a long-term investment vehicle.
Withdrawals / reimbursementsTax-free for qualified costsQualified reimbursements are not taxed. Unqualified or unused amounts can be lost or, if the plan allows, carried under tight rules.

For 2026, IRS Publication 15-B lists the health FSA salary-reduction limit as $3,400. Some plans permit a grace period or a limited carryover of unused health FSA amounts; the exact carryover dollar amount can change and is not quoted here because a 2026 figure was not confirmed from the official pages used for this collection. The dependent care exclusion is $7,500 ($3,750 if married filing separately) for 2026, as reflected in Publication 15-B and the IRS correction to the 2026 W-2/W-3 instructions, following the 2025 law (Public Law 119-21, §70404, often referred to as OBBBA). That is a household cap, not a per-child cap, and it is for qualifying care of a child under 13 or a disabled spouse or dependent.

Worked examples and scenarios

Example 1

A $3,400 health FSA in a 24% bracket

If Elena elects the full $3,400 2026 health FSA and spends it on qualified medical expenses, the federal income-tax savings in a 24% bracket are $3,400 × 0.24 = $816. If the election also reduces FICA wages, 7.65% of $3,400 is another $260 (rounded). Combined, that is about $1,076 of federal payroll-plus-income tax she does not pay on those dollars — provided she actually incurs $3,400 of qualified expenses. If she elects $3,400 and incurs only $1,000, the unused remainder can be forfeited unless her plan’s grace period or carryover (if any) covers it.

Federal income tax at 24%$816
FICA at 7.65%, if applicable$260

Example 2

Dependent care FSA at the 2026 $7,500 cap

Elena and her spouse both work. Qualifying after-school care will cost more than $7,500. A dependent care FSA election of $7,500 can exclude that amount from wages, subject to the earned-income tests and the plan’s substantiation rules. In a 24% federal bracket, $7,500 × 0.24 = $1,800 of federal income tax, before any payroll-tax effect. The same expenses generally cannot also generate a full child-and-dependent-care credit; the credit and the exclusion coordinate, and double-counting the same dollar is the usual mistake.

If married filing separately$3,750
Federal income tax at 24%$1,800

Example 3

Limited-purpose FSA beside an HSA

Elena switches mid-career to a qualifying HDHP and wants an HSA. A general-purpose health FSA would typically make her ineligible to contribute to the HSA. A limited-purpose FSA — dental and vision only, in the usual design — can sit beside the HSA. She could fund the 2026 HSA family limit (and catch-up, if she were 55+) and still elect a limited-purpose FSA for anticipated dental or vision costs, without using the FSA for the same medical expenses she intends to pay tax-free from the HSA.

General-purpose health FSA + HSAUsually not allowed
Limited-purpose FSA + HSAOften allowed
Dependent care FSA + HSACompatible; different expenses

Eligibility depends on the plan design and IRS HSA disqualifying-coverage rules. See the HSA story and IRS Publication 969.

Who it fits

  • Employees with predictable medical, dental, or vision spending who can elect a health or limited-purpose FSA and actually use it in the plan year.
  • Working parents or caregivers with qualifying dependent-care costs, up to $7,500 in 2026 ($3,750 if married filing separately).
  • HDHP enrollees who want a limited-purpose FSA alongside an HSA.

Who should skip — or wait

  • People whose medical spending is unpredictable enough that a large health FSA election would likely be forfeited.
  • Anyone who wants long-term tax-free growth — that is an HSA, Roth IRA, or 529 question, not an FSA question.
  • Households that would get more from the child and dependent care credit than from the exclusion, after running both.

Common mistakes

  • Electing a general-purpose health FSA and then trying to open or fund an HSA in the same coverage period.
  • Treating unused FSA money as something that automatically rolls into retirement. It generally does not.
  • Using the same dependent-care dollars for both the FSA exclusion and the full child-and-dependent-care credit.
  • Forgetting that a dependent care FSA is a household cap and has earned-income tests, not a per-child allowance.

Why this account matters

FSAs can be worth several hundred or several thousand dollars of tax a year — and can be worthless if the election is bigger than the expenses. They are tax-advantaged spending accounts, not tax-free investment accounts.
