Why Did Car Insurance Get So Expensive After COVID — and What Can You Actually Do?

Your bill jumped. Your driving didn't. Here is what actually happened after 2020, why insurers lost money and then overcorrected, and what an ordinary household can do now.

8 minute read November 18, 2026
“Fortnite Crew: $11.99 per month… Car Insurance: $157.00 per month. I receive — Nothing.”

— @wdelii on X, Oct 28 (~200,000 views, 10,500+ likes)

Another viral post, from @fwtimini (1.2M views, 20,800 likes): “Car insurance needs to be paid back at the end of the year if you didn't get into an accident.”

Millions of people feel the same thing: my bill jumped, my driving didn't. So here's the question: what actually happened after 2020, who's to blame, and what can an ordinary household do?

1. The jump, in numbers

Since January 2019, the government's car-insurance price index is up 49%. Overall inflation is up 34%. Car repair is up 62%. (BLS CPI, Jan 2019–Oct 2026; author's calculation, chart below) The sequence matters: used-car prices spiked first (up 54% by mid-2022), then repair prices climbed, then insurance followed with a lag.

2. Cause #1 — Fewer crashes, but each one costs far more

Per Verisk/ISO data published by Triple-I, from 2019 to 2024 collision claims became 17% less frequent, but the average claim rose from $3,828 to $5,489 (+43%). Property damage to others: +56% per claim. Net: claims cost per insured car rose about 19–27% on the main coverages (author's calculation: frequency × severity), and 70% on comprehensive (theft, hail, glass), where claims also got more frequent.

The new-angle culprit: your car's sensors. Cameras and radar now sit in mirrors, bumpers and windshields. AAA found driver-assist parts and calibration made up 71% of a $1,507 side-mirror repair and 41% of a minor rear-end repair. CCC says calibration now appears on 29.9% of repairable claims (23.3% a year earlier), and only 47% of those were on the first estimate. The average repair is $4,821, and 23.5% of appraised vehicles are now total losses.

3. Cause #2 — Injury claims and lawyers

Bodily-injury claims got 48% costlier per claim (ISO, same source). The Insurance Research Council found injury payments rose 7.8% a year from 2017 to 2022, more than double medical inflation. Lawyer involvement went from 40% to nearly 50% of claimants, and lawsuits nearly doubled (10% to 18%).

A twist most articles skip: represented claimants waited about 440 days (twice as long) and netted less per medical dollar ($1.40 vs $1.80). Triple-I estimates “legal system abuse” added $102–120 billion over 2016–2025 (about 9–11% of liability losses). Caveat: the IRC and Triple-I are insurer-funded, and “abuse” is a judgment call; the claim-cost rise itself shows up in the data either way.

4. The “are they gouging us?” test

Two things are true at once.

Insurers really did lose money. Their combined ratio (claims + expenses per premium dollar; above 100 = loss) went from 98.8 in 2019 to 112.2 in 2022 (Triple-I/NAIC data; author's calculation weighting liability and collision/comprehensive). They'd also handed back $14 billion+ in 2020 when we drove less. The catch-up hikes of 2022–24 were real.

But the catch-up overshot. By 2025 the ratio was 91.9: about 8 cents of underwriting profit per premium dollar, versus about 1 cent in 2019 (author's calculation; excludes investment income). Premiums rose about 48% (2019–24) while claims cost per car rose 19–27% on main coverages. Not the same measure, and premiums also fund expenses, but the gap is why profits recovered.

Now the pass-back. Insurers stopped asking for increases on most customers: 91% of policies had increase requests in 2023, versus 24% in 2026. State Farm's median filed change this year is 0%; GEICO's is −1.2% (NerdWallet's analysis of state filings). State Farm says it cut rates in 40 states (~10% on average) and paid a $5 billion dividend (~$100 per vehicle). In Florida, Commissioner Mike Yaworsky secured $46.2M in excess-profit refunds from Infinity Auto.

Verdict: gouging doesn't explain 2021–23. Slow pass-back partly explains 2025–26: the index peaked in February 2026 and is down 4.7% since, but is still 49% above 2019.

5. What did not cause it (much)

  • Reckless driving. Road deaths per mile jumped to 1.38 in 2021, but NHTSA estimates 1.10 in 2025, below 2019. Crashes got pricier, not more common.
  • Tariffs (so far). CCC's repair average rose only 0.6% year on year in H1 2026. Parts prices are climbing (Enlyte: OEM +4.2% in 2025), so it's a risk to watch, not yet the cause.
  • Credit scores. They shape who pays more, not why everyone does. Georgia's incoming commissioner wants them banned; that post got 2.7M views, but it needs legislation.

6. What people are saying

1. “I pay, I get nothing.” The two posts above. Insurance is pooled, so no refund by default; the real refunds come from mutual dividends and Florida's excess-profit rule.

2. “I did nothing wrong.” @bianxxa_lpz: rate “going from $170 to $300 a month because i removed my ex from my policy!? i have not had an at fault car accident in over 6 years” (1.7K views, small but typical). @natfactor77: “As cars become safer, you would think so too should car insurance.”

3. “Show me the money.” Florida's regulator: “Over $46 million in excess profits… will be returned” (66K views). WFLA: AAA cutting Florida rates a third time this year (54K views). @BishupH: “I just got mine!” (State Farm dividend).

4. “Who's to blame?” Credit and ZIP codes (above) versus lawsuit-reform advocates pointing at lawyers. Evidence supports parts of both.

7. What to do, tied to what's actually driving your bill

  • Re-shop now (cause: rate catch-up fading). Only 22% of insured Americans shopped in the past year (NerdWallet). With filings flat or falling, your renewal may not have been cut. Ask whether your renewal price beats a new-customer quote; Texas, Michigan, West Virginia and Washington all said in 2026 that insurers may not charge you more for not shopping.
  • Raise the deductible (cause: pricey repairs). Use money you could actually cover.
  • Check the car before you buy it (cause: ADAS). Get an insurance quote on the exact model and trim first.
  • Try telematics, with eyes open. Consumer Reports found a median saving of $120/year; some programs can raise rates, and they track your driving.
  • Old car? Compare your collision premium to what the car is worth, since CCC says 23.5% of appraised vehicles are now total losses. Don't drop liability.

Illustrative monthly example

Median premium $2,359/yr ≈ $197/mo (NerdWallet): a $1,000 deductible (NerdWallet: 12.8% cheaper) saves ≈ $302/yr. Subtract expected cost: ISO says a collision or comprehensive claim hits ~8.1% of cars a year (4.16% + 3.95%), so ≈ $41/yr (8.1% × $500, author's calculation). Net ≈ $22/mo. Add telematics at the median ($10/mo) and a 10% cut (State Farm's average, as a stand-in; ≈ $20/mo). Total up to ≈ $52/mo, but these don't stack perfectly, and your quotes will differ.

What we can't say for sure

The claims data (ISO) and the price data (CPI) measure different things, so “premiums outran claims” is a direction, not an exact split. The 2025 profit numbers are early, and no one has published 2026 results yet. Several key sources (Triple-I, the IRC, CCC, State Farm) are insurers or industry groups, so I've flagged where the claim is theirs. Your own state, car and record can easily beat or miss every average here.

Bottom line: your premium went up because claims got pricier, insurers lost money, then overcorrected. The cheapest fix right now is to make them compete for you.
