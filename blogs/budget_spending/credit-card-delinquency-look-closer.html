Credit-Card Delinquency Just Hit Great Recession Levels. Look Closer.

The scariest chart in consumer finance is, to a large extent, measuring the past, not the present. New delinquencies have been roughly flat for about two years.

Picture someone who fell behind on a credit card in the spring of 2023. Work hours got cut, the minimums slipped, and by the summer the account was three months late. Early the next year, the bank gave up collecting and wrote the balance off its own books. The borrower still owed the money — but as far as the bank's own delinquency numbers were concerned, that debt was gone.

Except it wasn't gone. The bank kept reporting it to the credit bureaus, and the borrower kept living with it: a lower credit score, a harder time renting, a loan that cost more. And every quarter since, the Federal Reserve's headline delinquency number has counted that same old debt again — alongside every other old debt like it.

That is the story behind the scariest chart in consumer finance right now. The share of card balances 90 or more days past due has climbed from 7.6% to 12.8% — a level last seen in the Great Recession. It sounds like millions of households are newly falling behind. But the New York Fed's own economists looked at the same data and found something else: the rate at which households are newly falling behind has been roughly flat for about two years. The headline number keeps climbing because old debts are sitting on credit reports for longer than they used to. Both facts are true. Only one of them describes what is happening to households right now.

1. The chart that launched a thousand doom posts

Every quarter the New York Fed publishes its report on household debt, built from a national sample of Equifax credit data. In August 2026 its economists put the alarming number in plain terms: between the third quarter of 2022 and the first quarter of 2026, the share of credit-card balances 90 or more days past due rose from 7.6% to 12.8%. That is the figure behind the Great Recession comparisons, and it is real — it is computed from the same credit-report data the Fed publishes every quarter, and nobody at the New York Fed disputes it.

But the same analysis points to a second number that almost never makes the headline. The flow rate — the share of balances that newly became 90 or more days past due in the quarter — levelled off in early 2024 and has stayed there. A third measure, drawn from banks' own reports to the Fed, tells the same calmer story.

So here is the contradiction. One number says delinquency has climbed by two-thirds in three and a half years. Two other numbers say the rate at which households are newly falling behind stopped rising in early 2024. They cannot all be describing the same thing — and they are not. The first number counts old debts. The other two count new ones.

2. The pile and the tap

The difference is simple once you see it. Think of a bath. The pile is the water already in the tub. The tap is the water still running in. The headline number measures the water in the tub: every dollar of card balance that is 90 or more days late, no matter how old the lateness is. The quieter number measures only the tap: the balances that newly went seriously late in that quarter.

Now follow one debt through the system — the same journey millions of old debts have taken. Say you stopped paying your card in the spring of 2023. After three months the account was seriously late. After four to six months, the bank charged it off: it wrote the balance off its own books and stopped counting it in its own delinquency figures. But you still owed the money, and the bank could keep reporting the debt to the credit bureaus. That is the key step. The debt leaves the bank's books, but it does not leave your credit report. It can sit there for up to seven years.

Here is where the two numbers split apart. When new delinquencies surged after the pandemic, the pile and the tap rose together. When the pace of new delinquencies levelled off in early 2024, the tap stopped rising — but the pile kept growing, because old charged-off debts were piling up faster than they were falling off reports.

The New York Fed's breakdown is blunt about the cause. The recent rise in the headline rate, its economists found, "has been driven almost entirely by a growing pool of severely derogatory (charged off) balances". And the reason that pool is growing is, in large part, that lenders now report these debts for longer. Between 2004 and 2012, only about 40% of charged-off debts were still being reported a year later. By 2024, that figure had doubled to 80%. The debts didn't get worse. They just stayed visible twice as long.

3. Flat is not the same as fine

None of this means households are doing well. The New York Fed's own summary is careful: by the flow measures, "the pace of credit card delinquency is elevated but has been largely stable since 2024". Elevated. Stable. Both words are doing work — and the first one matters more than the second.

The precise numbers make the point. In the second quarter of 2026, the annualised rate at which card balances newly went seriously late was 6.97%, against 6.93% a year earlier — essentially unchanged, and high. Total household debt stood at $18.771 trillion at the end of June 2026, with card balances at $1.263 trillion, and 4.7% of all outstanding household debt was in some stage of delinquency. These are not the numbers of a consumer in rude health.

There is also the counterweight from the Federal Reserve's own Survey of Consumer Finances, covered in this space last week: among families holding debt, 19.6% reported a late loan payment in the preceding year, up from 12.2% in 2022. That is a different measure — a survey answer about any late payment, not a credit-report transition — and it fits the flow story if most of the damage happened in 2023 and early 2024 and then levelled off. But it is a reminder that this correction is about the direction of travel now, not a claim that nothing went wrong.

And there is a new number worth watching, released just three days before this piece: the Fed's G.19 release for September 2026 shows revolving credit — mostly card balances — growing at a 14.5% annual rate in the month, after shrinking in August, taking outstanding revolving credit to $1,368.5 billion. The rate on card accounts assessed interest reached 22.36% in the third quarter. One month does not make a trend, and rising balances reflect spending as well as distress. But borrowing more at 22% is the pipeline into future delinquency — and it is the series to watch next.

4. Why the pile still hurts

It would be a mistake to dismiss the headline number entirely, and the New York Fed does not. The pile is real debt owed by real people: more than 23 million Americans still have charged-off credit-card balances on their credit reports. A charged-off balance can sit on a report for years, depressing a credit score, raising the cost of the next loan, and in some cases blocking a rental application or a job that checks credit. The fact that the debt is old does not make it harmless to the person carrying it. It makes it a different kind of problem: not a wave of new defaults, but a long tail of households still living with the defaults of 2023 and 2024.

There is a second reason the pile matters. Lenders changed how long they report these debts, and the New York Fed's authors are candid that they do not fully know why. One possibility is that lenders are having less success collecting, leaving more debts outstanding — though the recovery rates reported by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau have "only changed slightly", which the authors say makes that explanation unlikely to be important. The alternative is that reporting practices changed in a way "uncorrelated with debt performance". Either way, a chart that mixes a behaviour change with a distress change will mislead anyone who reads it as a pure distress signal.

The delinquency rate did hit Great Recession levels. It just isn't measuring what the headline says it is measuring.

5. What an ordinary household should actually do differently

Check your own report for the pile. If you fell behind in 2023 or 2024, a charged-off balance may still be sitting on your report even though the account is closed and the lender has moved on. You can get your reports free, and errors in charged-off balances are worth disputing — the balance, the date of first delinquency, and whether it is marked as settled all affect your score and how long the item lingers.

Know which kind of late you are. If you are trying to judge whether households like yours are newly struggling, watch the flow — new delinquencies — not the pile. The next New York Fed quarterly report is the one to check, not the latest retelling of the 12.8% figure.

Old debt is negotiable in a way a current balance is not. Lenders and collectors often settle old charged-off debts for less than the full amount, and getting a settlement in writing — including how it will be reported — matters more than the size of any single payment. Do not let a collector's urgency set your timetable; the debt is old, and a rushed payment you cannot sustain helps no one.

Don't let a scary headline move your money. A chart that says delinquency is at Great Recession levels invites exactly the wrong response — selling investments, or freezing a sensible plan — if the underlying flow of new distress has been flat for two years. The question for your household is narrower and more useful: are you newly late, or are you carrying old damage? They need different fixes.

Watch the tap, not the tub. The September jump in revolving credit, at a 22.36% average rate on accounts assessed interest, is the number that could turn into next year's delinquency flow. If your own card balance is growing month to month while your income is not, that is the early warning that matters — and it is visible on your statement long before it shows up in any Fed chart.

Signals to watch

The New York Fed's next Quarterly Report on Household Debt and Credit — the flow into serious delinquency for credit cards, and whether the stock rate keeps climbing on old balances. The Fed's G.19 release for October 2026, due in early December — does the September revolving jump persist or reverse? And home equity lines of credit, up for a 17th consecutive quarter to $459 billion in the second quarter of 2026: the pressure valve households reach for when card borrowing gets expensive, and a risk of its own if it becomes the way monthly shortfalls get funded.

Conclusion

The delinquency rate did hit Great Recession levels. It just isn't measuring what the headline says it is measuring. The pile of old debt keeps growing; the tap of new distress has been steady for two years. Know which one you are looking at — and which one you are living in.
