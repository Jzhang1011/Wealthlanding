America Got Richer. The Bottom Got Poorer.

The average American family has never been worth more on paper. But the gains went to the top of the pyramid — and the families at the bottom lost ground, then got the bill anyway.

The average American family has never been worth more on paper. Mean net worth rose 7% to about $1.24 million. But averages hide who got it. The typical family's net worth rose just 2%, to $215,900 — and at the bottom of the pyramid, families lost more than half of what little they had.

If you rent, or you are under 35, or you never finished high school, the last three years probably did not feel like a wealth boom. House prices kept climbing. The monthly bills kept climbing. And the wealth that was supposedly being created went mostly to people who already owned the things that were going up in value. That is what the Federal Reserve's newest Survey of Consumer Finances shows, once you look past the headline: more wealth in total, but only the top of the pyramid got it.

1. The pyramid: who gained, who lost

Between the Fed's 2022 and 2025 surveys, the split ran cleanly down the income ladder. Families in the lowest fifth of income saw their median net worth fall 6%. Families in the middle, between the 40th and 80th percentiles, saw it grow 5 to 15%. Families in the top tenth saw their median net worth jump 31%, to $3,669,900.

Cut the same families by how much wealth they already had, and the split is starker. The bottom quarter's median net worth fell from $3,800 to $1,700 — a 55% drop, leaving the typical family at the bottom with less than two thousand dollars to its name, debts included. The top tenth's median net worth rose 19%, to $4,935,200. The Fed's own words: the top tenth's wealth was "approximately four to six times that of the next highest percentile group, reflecting concentration of wealth at the top of the distribution."

This is a change from the last cycle, not a continuation of it. Between 2019 and 2022, net worth grew broadly across the distribution — most groups gained. Between 2022 and 2025, the bottom lost and the top gained. The same pattern shows up everywhere you cut the data. Renters lost 7% of median net worth, down to $10,600, while homeowners gained 6%, to $458,900. Families under 35 lost 23%, down to $33,000. Families with no high school diploma lost 55% of median net worth — their largest fall in the modern survey — while the average within that same group rose 53%, which means the gap widened even inside the group at the bottom. Black families lost 25% of median net worth while the average rose 5%; the typical White family's wealth is now nine times the typical Black family's, the widest ratio since 2016.

2. The curve tilted

You can see the same story in a single picture: the net worth of the family at each point on the ladder. At the 25th percentile — a family one-quarter of the way up — net worth fell 6%, to $27,900. At the middle, the median, it rose 2%, to $215,900. At the 75th percentile it rose 13%, to $812,800. At the 90th percentile it rose 20%, to $2,539,200. The bottom of the curve went down. The top of the curve went up. The middle barely moved.

One thing this is not: an income story. The typical family's income did rise 7% in real terms, to $82,200. But the top's income actually fell — the 90th percentile of usual income dropped 7.5%, to $248,500, and average income fell 6% largely because the top fell. Exclude the top 1% and average income fell only 1%. The split is in wealth, in what families own, not in what they earn. That matters, because wealth is what cushions a bad month — and the cushion is exactly what the bottom lost.

3. Paper wealth has a price

Here is the part that stings. The wealth at the top did not appear from nowhere. House prices rose 12% over the same period, on the CoreLogic index. For the two-thirds of families who own a home, that was a paper gain: the median home's net value — price minus mortgage — rose 5%, to $230,000, and homeowners' median net worth rose 6%. For the one-third who do not own, there was no gain to offset anything. Homeownership was flat at 66%, and the top tenth's homeownership rate actually rose, from 89% to 92%.

So the same price rise that made owners richer on paper made life more expensive for everyone else. A renter watching prices climb does not get a wealthier balance sheet; they get a higher bar to clear and a higher rent to pay. And renters' median net worth fell 7% over the same three years, to $10,600. The paper wealth and the inflation that comes with it are the same event, seen from opposite sides of the ownership line.

4. And the bill still comes due

None of this would matter as much if the monthly bills had stayed manageable. They did not. Among families holding debt, 19.6% said they had been behind on a loan payment in the preceding year, up from 12.2% in 2022 — the highest reading in the survey's recent table. The share late by two months or more rose from 4.9% to 8.2%. The median payment-to-income ratio rose from 13.4% to 15.4%, and the share of all families devoting more than 40% of income to debt payments rose from 6.5% to 8.6%.

Two honest limits belong here. First, the survey does not break late payments down by income group, so I cannot prove from this survey alone that the families who lost wealth are the same ones falling behind. The pattern points the same way — less cushion, more missed payments — but the survey gives me the two facts side by side, not the overlap. Second, this is not a story of mass collapse: bankruptcy in the previous five years was largely unchanged at 1.4%, and foreclosure proceedings edged up only from 0.5% to 0.7%. What grew was the squeezed middle — families missing payments and carrying a heavier monthly load, not families losing everything at once.

It is also worth saying plainly that the strain is showing up in how sure people feel, not just in what they owe. The share of families who said they did not have a good idea of their income for the next year rose to 37%, a new high in the survey. When the bottom of the pyramid is thinner and the future is foggier, a tight month turns into a missed payment faster.

The country got richer. The wealth just went to the top of the pyramid — and the bill for the higher prices it created landed on everyone else.

5. What an ordinary household should actually do differently

First, know your number. Add up what you own minus what you owe — that is your net worth, and it is the number that tells you how much cushion you actually have. If it is close to zero or negative, you are in the group the survey says lost ground, and the priority is the buffer, not the return on it.

Second, run the ratio. Add up every required monthly loan payment — mortgage, car, cards, student and personal loans — and divide by monthly income. The Fed's 40% line uses before-tax income and excludes rent and vehicle leases, so your own all-in figure, rent included, will look higher; do not panic at a direct comparison, but do notice if your figure has crept up even though your total debt has not. That creep is the pattern the survey found.

Third, split your wealth in two. Treat asset wealth and bill-paying cash as separate accounts in your head, because the survey shows they behave separately. A higher home value is not a payment plan. If your buffer lives entirely in equity or a retirement account, a single expensive month can still force a late payment, with fees and credit consequences that equity cannot undo retroactively.

Fourth, call before you're late. If a payment is at risk, act before it is late rather than after. Call the servicer while you are still current and ask about hardship options, due-date changes, or a revised payment schedule. A late payment in the survey's sense is any slip in the preceding year, and the cheapest late payment is the one that never happens.

Finally, build the buffer. Protect it before the next expensive surprise. The practical lesson of a 19.6% late-payment share is not that everyone is in trouble; it is that trouble arrived for families whose incomes and wealth were, on average, rising. A small, boring cash reserve — separate from investments, accessible without selling anything — is what turns a tight month into an anecdote instead of a missed payment.

Signals to watch

The next Bureau of Economic Analysis release on personal income and outlays, covering October 2026, is due on 25 November 2026. September's release already showed the same squeeze in aggregate form: the personal saving rate at 3.8% of disposable personal income, with real consumer spending up less than 0.1% in the month. That aggregate saving rate is a flow, not a typical family's bank balance, so it is a signal to watch, not proof of anything about any one household. The New York Fed's quarterly household debt report is the other series to follow; the Fed's own survey will not be repeated for three years, so the next read on whether the bottom recovers is a long way off.

Conclusion

The country got richer. The wealth just went to the top of the pyramid — and the bill for the higher prices it created landed on everyone else.
