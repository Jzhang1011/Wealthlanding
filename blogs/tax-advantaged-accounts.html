Tax-advantaged accounts, organized by who they fit

These accounts are tax-advantaged. They are not all tax-free. A traditional 401(k) or 403(b), and an FSA, give you a tax break when money goes in — and then follow their own rules on the way out. A Roth IRA, a Roth 401(k), an HSA used for qualified medical expenses, and a 529 used for qualified education expenses can be tax-free on qualified withdrawals. Insurance designs sit at the edge of the map, with conditions.

The 45–65 catch-up years

From 50 onward, several official limits increase — and a few eligibility clocks start running the other way. These are the 2026 figures this collection uses throughout.

Age 50+$8,000 / $1,100Workplace elective catch-up is $8,000 in 2026. The IRA catch-up is $1,100, for a $8,600 IRA limit.
Ages 60–63$11,250SECURE 2.0 super catch-up for 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457(b), and the TSP in 2026. It does not apply at age 64.
Roth catch-up rule$150,000If prior-year FICA wages from the plan sponsor exceeded $150,000 (the 2026 threshold), catch-ups must be Roth.
HSA at 55+$1,000 moreThe HSA catch-up is $1,000. Medicare enrollment — including Part A — stops HSA contributions.
Traditional RMDsStill applyTraditional 401(k), 403(b), and traditional IRA balances have required minimum distributions.
Roth 401(k) RMDsNone since 2024Designated Roth accounts in workplace plans have had no lifetime RMDs beginning 2024. Roth IRAs already had none for the original owner.

A sensible order — max these first

Insurance-based strategies and executive bonus plans are last for a reason. They can be appropriate after the statutory accounts are used, and they can be expensive distractions before that.

  1. Capture the workplace match.An employer match is an immediate return that no insurance illustration replaces.
  2. Fund an HSA if you are eligible.Pre-tax or deductible in, tax-free growth, tax-free qualified medical withdrawals.
  3. Use 401(k), 403(b), or solo 401(k) deferrals and catch-ups.Including the 2026 super catch-up at ages 60–63, and Roth catch-ups when the wage rule requires them.
  4. Add a Roth IRA — or a backdoor Roth if income requires it.A smaller cap, but tax-free qualified withdrawals and no lifetime RMDs for the original owner.
  5. Self-employed? Compare a SEP and a solo 401(k).The elective deferral is often the difference at moderate incomes.
  6. Then education accounts, if retirement is on track.A 529 for scalable saving; a Coverdell only if the $2,000 cap and income test fit.
  7. Only then consider an executive bonus plan or IUL.These are compensation or insurance designs with conditions, not substitutes for the accounts above.
