Guide

Understanding compound growth

Compound growth is the reason starting early, or simply staying invested, matters so much. This guide explains what the calculator does, how to use it, and how to read what it shows.

What this calculator does

It projects how a starting balance plus a fixed monthly contribution could grow if every dollar earns the same annual rate of return, compounded monthly. It splits the final balance into the money you put in (principal) and the growth earned on that money (interest or investment return).

How to use it

  1. Enter what you have invested today in Initial Investment.
  2. Enter what you plan to add each month in Monthly Contribution.
  3. Set Years to Grow to the time until you expect to start spending the money.
  4. Choose an Estimated Annual Return. Try a cautious, a middle, and a hopeful rate rather than a single number.
  5. Press Calculate Growth.

How to read the results

Future Balance is the projected total at the end of the period. Total Principal is everything you contributed. Total Interest is the difference, the part produced by compounding. The bar under the totals shows the same split as percentages. When the growth slice is larger than the principal slice, time has done more of the work than your deposits.

Assumptions and limits

  • The return is constant every month. Real markets move up and down, and the order of good and bad years matters, especially near retirement.
  • Results are in future dollars. Inflation is not subtracted, so a dollar at the end buys less than a dollar today. To think in today's dollars, enter a return that is already reduced by your inflation assumption.
  • Taxes, fund fees and account rules are not modeled.
  • The default 7% is an illustrative assumption, not a forecast or a promise.

Worked example with the default numbers

The calculator opens with $1,000 invested, $500 added every month, 30 years, and a 7% annual return. You would contribute $181,000 in total ($1,000 plus $500 × 12 months × 30 years). The projected balance is about $618,102, so roughly $437,102, about 71% of the total, comes from growth rather than deposits.

Change only the years from 30 to 20 and the projected balance falls to about $264,500, less than half, even though you would still contribute two thirds as much ($121,000). That gap is the cost of a shorter runway.
