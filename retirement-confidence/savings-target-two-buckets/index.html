How Much Should You Have Saved by 50?

Savings benchmarks can reassure or alarm. Here is what the best-known ones actually assume, how to read your own numbers calmly, and a simple way to separate money you will need soon from money meant to grow.

By the WealthLanding Knowledge GuardianLast reviewed: October 15, 20267 min read

Within your direct control: savings rate and spending

A composite story (not real people)

Mark and Dana are both 50. Between two workplace plans and an IRA they have about $400,000 saved, on a household income of $150,000. A headline told them they "should" have far more by now, and the weekend turned into an argument about whose fault that was.

Their real question is more useful than the headline: Are we on a reasonable path, and if not, what can we change?

The principle: a benchmark is a starting point, not a verdict

Savings benchmarks are helpful because they turn a vague worry into a number you can compare against. They are also easy to misuse, because every benchmark is built on assumptions that may not match your life. Two respected sources can give very different numbers for the same household, and both can be reasonable.

The useful habit is to read the assumptions first, then the number. If your situation differs (you plan to retire later, you have a pension, you expect to spend less) your target moves too.

What two widely used benchmarks say

J.P. Morgan's retirement savings checkpoints

J.P. Morgan Asset Management's 2026 Guide to Retirement publishes "retirement savings checkpoints": the amount a household might have saved today, by age and household income, to stay on track. Selected figures for age 50:

Fidelity's salary multiples

Fidelity suggests aiming for about 1x your salary saved by 30, 3x by 40, 6x by 50, 8x by 60 and 10x by 67. Its assumptions differ: saving 15% of income every year from age 25 (including any employer match), retiring at 67, planning through age 93, replacing about 45% of pre-retirement income from savings, and assuming no pension. Fidelity describes these milestones as aspirational and notes that many people will not meet all of them.

Why the numbers differ. For a 50-year-old household earning $150,000, Fidelity's 6x points to about $900,000, while J.P. Morgan's checkpoint is $615,000. The gap comes from different retirement ages, savings rates, return assumptions, confidence levels and income-replacement goals. Neither is "wrong." Each answers a slightly different question.

If you are behind: assess without panic

Being below a benchmark is common and it is information, not a judgment. A calm assessment has four parts:

  1. Know your real numbers. Total retirement savings, current savings rate (including any employer match) and an estimate of what you will spend in retirement.
  2. Count your other income. Your Social Security estimate (available in a personal my Social Security account), any pension, rental income or planned part-time work all reduce how much your savings must provide.
  3. Identify your levers. Saving more, working a little longer, spending somewhat less in retirement and claiming Social Security later each move the target. Small changes to several levers often add up to more than a big change to one.
  4. Use the catch-up rules. For 2026 the IRS allows employees 50 and older to add an $8,000 catch-up contribution to most 401(k), 403(b) and governmental 457 plans, on top of the $24,500 regular limit, for a total of $32,500. Workers aged 60 to 63 may have a higher catch-up of $11,250 if their plan allows it. The IRA limit is $7,500, or $8,600 at 50 and older.

The two-bucket approach: separate near-term needs from long-term growth

As retirement approaches, a second question appears: how do you invest money you will start spending soon without giving up the growth you need for later? A simple way to think about it is two buckets.

Two buckets at a glance
Cash-flow bucketGrowth bucket
PurposePays the next few years of spending that income does not coverFunds spending many years from now and keeps pace with inflation
Typical holdingsCash, high-yield savings, short-term high-quality bonds or similarA diversified mix that usually includes stocks
What it protects againstHaving to sell growth investments after a market dropInflation eroding purchasing power over decades
How it is refilledFrom income, interest and dividends, and periodically from the growth bucket when markets allowLong-term returns

Illustration: a couple expects to spend $90,000 a year in retirement and estimates $48,000 a year of combined Social Security. The gap their savings must cover is $42,000 a year. Holding two to three years of that gap in the cash-flow bucket would mean roughly $84,000 to $126,000. This is an example, not a rule.

How large your cash-flow bucket should be depends on your spending, how much of it is covered by reliable income, your comfort with market swings and the rest of your plan. A larger reserve can make downturns easier to live through, but money held in cash grows slowly, so a bucket that is too large has its own cost.

What you can do this month

  • Add up your current retirement savings and your annual savings rate, including any employer match.
  • Download your Social Security estimate and note your full-retirement-age benefit.
  • Write down an approximate retirement spending figure in today's dollars.
  • Check whether you are using catch-up contributions if you are 50 or older.
  • Decide roughly how many years of spending you would want in a cash-flow reserve once you retire.
  • If you are behind, choose one lever to adjust now and one to revisit next year.

Common mistakes

Treating a benchmark as pass or fail

Benchmarks average across many households. Yours may need more or less. Use the assumptions to adjust the number, not to grade yourself.

Comparing your single number with the wrong row

J.P. Morgan's checkpoints use gross household income; Fidelity's multiples use your salary. Make sure you compare like with like.

Waiting for the "right time" to increase savings

The years between 50 and retirement often coincide with peak earnings and higher contribution limits. Delaying an increase by a few years reduces the time that money has to grow.

Keeping everything in one bucket

Investing all of your savings the same way, either all cash or all growth, ignores the fact that some money is needed soon and some may not be touched for 20 years.

When individual circumstances change the answer

  • You have a pension or other guaranteed income. The benchmarks above generally assume none, so your savings target may be lower.
  • You plan to retire early or late. Fidelity notes that retiring at 65 rather than 67 raises its target to about 12x salary, while retiring at 70 lowers it to about 8x.
  • Your spending will change significantly. Paying off a mortgage, downsizing or relocating can reduce what you need; extensive travel or supporting family can increase it.
  • Your health or family situation. Expected healthcare costs, caregiving responsibilities and the needs of a surviving spouse all matter more than any average.

Questions readers ask

How much should I have saved by 50?

It depends on your income, retirement age, expected spending and other income. J.P. Morgan's 2026 checkpoints suggest about $430,000 for a 50-year-old household earning $100,000 (assuming 10% savings from now on, retirement at 65 and other stated assumptions). Fidelity suggests about 6x your salary by 50 under its own assumptions. Treat both as starting points.

What if I am far behind the benchmarks?

Many people are. Focus on the levers you control: raising your savings rate, using catch-up contributions, working a little longer if possible, adjusting expected retirement spending and claiming Social Security strategically. Several modest changes often add up.

How many years of spending should be in the cash-flow bucket?

There is no universal number. Some people hold one to three years of the spending their income does not cover; others hold more or less. The right size depends on your spending, reliable income, risk tolerance and overall plan.
