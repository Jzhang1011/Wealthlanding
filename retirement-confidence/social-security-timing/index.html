Principle 04 · Social Security Timing

Should You Claim Social Security at 62, 67, or 70?

Claim early and get more checks; wait and get bigger ones. Here is how the rules actually work, what the breakeven math shows and misses, and why couples should think about the survivor first.

By the WealthLanding Knowledge GuardianLast reviewed: October 15, 20266 min read

A composite story (not real people)

Maria turns 62 next spring. She was born in 1965, and her Social Security statement estimates a monthly benefit of $2,400 at her full retirement age of 67. Her husband Tom, two years older, earned less and expects about $1,100 at his full retirement age. A coworker told Maria to "take it at 62 before the system runs out." Her brother says "always wait until 70." Both say it with total confidence.

The truth is that the right age depends on things only Maria and Tom can weigh: their health, their other savings, whether they are still working and what happens to the survivor when one of them dies.

The principle: claiming age is a decision you can influence

You cannot control future legislation or how long you will live. You can choose when to start benefits, and that choice permanently changes the size of every monthly check. Claiming early means more checks that are each smaller; claiming later means fewer checks that are each larger.

How the benefit changes with claiming age

According to the Social Security Administration, you can start retirement benefits as early as 62, but you receive your full benefit only at your full retirement age (FRA). For anyone born in 1960 or later, FRA is 67. Claiming at 62 permanently reduces the benefit to about 70% of the full amount, a 30% reduction. Delaying past FRA earns delayed retirement credits of 8% a year (two-thirds of 1% a month) for people born in 1943 or later, and the credits stop at 70. For someone with an FRA of 67, waiting until 70 produces about 124% of the full amount.

SSA notes that percentages are approximate due to rounding, that you must be 62 for the entire month to receive benefits, and that people born on the first of a month are treated as if born in the previous month. Annual cost-of-living adjustments apply to whichever amount you start with.

In this simplified example, claiming at 67 overtakes claiming at 62 at about age 78 years and 8 months. Claiming at 70 overtakes 62 at about 80 years and 4 months, and overtakes 67 at about 82 and a half.

Breakeven is not the whole story. A breakeven age tells you when the totals cross. It does not tell you what matters most for many retirees: whether the household still has enough income if one or both spouses live into their 90s. Delaying can be viewed as buying a larger, inflation-adjusted income for a long life, which is the risk that is hardest to cover any other way.

Spousal and survivor benefits change the math for couples

For married couples, the decision is really two decisions that affect each other.

  • Spousal benefit. SSA explains that a spouse's full benefit can be up to one-half of the worker's full-retirement-age amount. It is reduced if the spouse claims before their own FRA, and it does not grow with the worker's delayed retirement credits. If you qualify for both your own benefit and a spousal benefit, you are deemed to apply for both and receive the higher combined amount.
  • Survivor benefit. When one spouse dies, the survivor generally keeps the larger of the two benefits. SSA notes that a surviving spouse's benefit is based on the deceased worker's higher amount, including delayed retirement credits.

For Maria and Tom, that last point matters most. If Maria, the higher earner, waits to 70, her $2,976 (in today's dollars) is not only her income; it is also the amount Tom could receive as a survivor if he outlives her. Tom's own claiming age affects his check but has less effect on the survivor's income, which is why some couples have the lower earner claim earlier and the higher earner claim later.

Bridging the gap if you delay

Delaying only works if you can cover your spending in the meantime. Common bridges include:

  • Continued work, full or part time. Note that if you claim before FRA while working, SSA may withhold benefits: for 2026 it deducts $1 for every $2 earned above $24,480, with a higher limit of $65,160 in the year you reach FRA. Withheld amounts are credited back after FRA through a recalculation.
  • Withdrawals from savings, often from traditional retirement accounts during lower-income years, which can also reduce future required minimum distributions. See Principle 07.
  • A cash-flow reserve set aside specifically for the bridge years, as described in Principle 02.

Whatever you decide about Social Security, SSA reminds people who delay past 65 to still apply for Medicare within three months of their 65th birthday, because late enrollment can cost more.

What you can do

  • Create or sign in to a my Social Security account and download your statement; check your earnings record for errors.
  • Note your full retirement age and your estimated benefit at 62, FRA and 70.
  • If married, write down both benefits and consider which one would continue for the survivor.
  • Estimate how you would cover spending in any years between retiring and claiming.
  • Think honestly about health and family longevity, while remembering that averages are not limits.
  • Revisit the decision before you file. You do not have to decide years in advance.

Common misconceptions

"I should claim early before Social Security runs out."

Questions about the program's long-term finances are real, and future legislation is outside your control. But claiming early locks in a permanently smaller benefit based on a guess about policy. Many planners treat policy risk as a reason to keep flexibility, not as a reason to claim at 62 by default.

"Waiting until 70 is always best."

Delaying increases the monthly amount, but it is not right for everyone. Poor health, no savings to bridge the gap, or a need for income now can make an earlier claim reasonable.

"My spouse's benefit grows if I delay."

The spousal benefit is capped at half of your full-retirement-age amount and does not grow with delayed credits. The survivor benefit, however, does reflect your delayed credits.

"Breakeven age is all I need."

Breakeven ignores the value of insurance against a long life, the needs of a surviving spouse and the effect of taxes.

When individual circumstances change the answer

  • Divorced spouses married at least 10 years may qualify for benefits on a former spouse's record.
  • Pensions from work not covered by Social Security, disability benefits and survivor benefits each have their own rules.
  • Taxes. Part of your benefit may be taxable depending on your other income, which interacts with withdrawal and Roth conversion plans.
  • Your own numbers. Use SSA's personal estimates rather than averages; your earnings history and birth date determine the exact figures.

Questions readers ask

How much is my Social Security reduced if I claim at 62?

For people born in 1960 or later, whose full retirement age is 67, SSA's table shows that claiming at 62 reduces the benefit to about 70% of the full amount, a 30% reduction. The reduction is permanent.

How much more do I get by waiting until 70?

For people born in 1943 or later, SSA adds delayed retirement credits of 8% a year after full retirement age, stopping at 70. With a full retirement age of 67, claiming at 70 gives about 124% of the full benefit.

Does my spouse's benefit increase if I delay?

The spousal benefit is up to half of your full-retirement-age amount and does not include delayed credits. A survivor benefit, however, is based on your higher amount including delayed credits, which is why delaying can help a surviving spouse.
