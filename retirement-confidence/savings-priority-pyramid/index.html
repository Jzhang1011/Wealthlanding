Where Should Your Next Dollar of Savings Go?

Emergency fund, employer match, credit card, Roth IRA: when everything feels urgent, an order helps. Here is a practical framework for deciding where your next dollar goes, and when to bend it.

By the WealthLanding Knowledge GuardianLast reviewed: October 15, 20266 min read

Within your direct control: savings rate and account choices

A composite story (not a real person)

Paul is 54 and earns $90,000. He just received a $10,000 bonus after taxes. He also carries $8,000 on a credit card at 22% interest, has about $3,000 in savings, and contributes 3% of his pay to a 401(k) whose employer match he has never fully claimed. Friends have told him to "max out the Roth," "pay off the card," and "buy index funds." All three sound sensible. Which comes first?

The principle: give every dollar a priority

When everything feels important, it helps to have an order. The Savings Priority Pyramid is a way to decide where your next dollar of savings goes. The wide base is what most people should secure first; each higher level builds on the ones below.

It is a framework, not a rule. Plans differ, debts differ and family situations differ. But having a default order turns a stressful decision into a short checklist.

The seven levels, from the base up

  1. Emergency reserves. Cash you can reach quickly for job loss, repairs or medical bills. A common rule of thumb is three to six months of essential expenses; people closer to retirement, or with less stable income, often prefer more. Without a reserve, a surprise bill can push you onto a credit card or into an early retirement-plan withdrawal.
  2. The full employer match. If your employer matches contributions, contributing enough to receive the full match is often the highest-value step available, because the match is extra compensation you only receive if you contribute. Check your plan's vesting schedule.
  3. High-cost debt. Paying down debt with a high interest rate, such as most credit cards, is a guaranteed "return" equal to the rate you stop paying. Lower-rate debts like many mortgages are a judgment call, not an emergency.
  4. A Health Savings Account, if you are eligible. HSAs are available only with a qualifying high-deductible health plan. IRS Publication 969 explains the tax treatment: contributions can be deductible, growth is not taxed while in the account, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. For 2026 the limits are $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage, plus $1,000 more if you are 55 or older. You cannot contribute once you are enrolled in Medicare.
  5. Additional workplace retirement contributions. Beyond the match, a 401(k), 403(b) or similar plan lets you save more with tax advantages. For 2026 the employee limit is $24,500, plus an $8,000 catch-up at 50 or older, or a higher $11,250 catch-up at ages 60 to 63 if your plan allows it.
  6. An IRA. A traditional or Roth IRA adds more tax-advantaged room: $7,500 for 2026, or $8,600 if you are 50 or older, limited to your taxable compensation. Roth IRA contributions phase out at higher incomes, and deductions for traditional IRA contributions can be limited if you have a workplace plan.
  7. Taxable investing. A regular brokerage account has no contribution limits and no age restrictions on withdrawals. It does not have the tax advantages of retirement accounts, but it offers flexibility, which can be valuable for early retirement or large one-time goals.

A worked example: Paul's $10,000

Assumptions (hypothetical): salary $90,000; the plan matches 50% of employee contributions up to 6% of pay; $8,000 credit-card balance at 22% APR; $3,000 in savings; essential expenses about $4,000 a month; Paul is not enrolled in an HSA-eligible plan. Interest figures are simplified to one year without compounding.

1. ReserveMoves part of the bonus into savings so his reserve covers at least a few months of essentialsPrevents the next surprise from going back on the card

Paul's friends were each partly right. The order simply tells him which advice to apply first, given his situation.

Why the match comes before the debt here. A 50% match on contributions is an immediate return on those dollars, before any investment growth. That is larger than the 22% interest he saves by paying down the card. For someone with no match, or debt at a higher rate, the order between levels 2 and 3 might change.

Trade-offs that can change the order

Factors that may move a level up or down
FactorHow it can change the order
LiquidityRetirement accounts can carry taxes and penalties on early withdrawals. If you may need the money before retirement, a larger reserve or taxable account may deserve priority.
Plan rulesMatching formulas, vesting schedules, investment choices and fees differ. A plan with very high fees may make an IRA more attractive after the match.
Debt costsThe higher the interest rate, the stronger the case for paying it down early. Low fixed-rate debt rarely needs to come before retirement saving.
EligibilityHSAs require an HSA-eligible health plan and end with Medicare enrollment. Roth IRA contributions depend on income. Catch-up contributions depend on age and plan rules.
Early retirementIf you plan to stop working well before 60, money you can reach without penalty, such as a taxable account, may need a higher place in the order.

What you can do

  • Write down your current emergency reserve in months of essential expenses.
  • Look up your plan's matching formula and confirm you receive the full match.
  • List every debt with its interest rate, highest first.
  • Check whether your health plan is HSA-eligible, and whether you are using it.
  • If you are 50 or older, confirm whether you are using catch-up contributions.
  • Decide where the next $100 a month will go, using the pyramid as a default.

Common mistakes

Skipping the reserve to invest faster

Without cash on hand, an unexpected expense often ends up on a credit card or forces an early retirement-plan withdrawal, which can undo months of progress.

Leaving the match on the table

Contributing less than the amount needed for the full match means giving up part of your compensation.

Treating all debt the same

A 22% credit card and a 3% mortgage are very different problems. Paying low-rate debt early can feel good but may delay higher-value steps.

Using an HSA only as a spending account

Some people pay current medical bills from other funds and leave HSA money invested for future healthcare costs. Whether that suits you depends on your cash flow and plan.

When individual circumstances change the answer

  • Unstable income or a single income household. A larger emergency reserve may come first.
  • Very high-interest or collection debt. Addressing it may outrank even the match.
  • Supporting family members. Cash-flow needs may require more liquidity.
  • Tax situation. Whether traditional or Roth contributions make more sense depends on your current and expected future tax rates; see Principle 06.
  • Limits change each year. The 2026 figures above come from the IRS; check irs.gov for the current year before contributing.

Questions readers ask

Should I pay off debt or save for retirement first?

It depends on the interest rate and whether you have an employer match. Many people contribute enough to receive the full match, then focus on high-interest debt such as credit cards, then increase retirement savings. Low-rate debt usually does not need to come first.

What are the 2026 contribution limits?

According to the IRS, for 2026 the 401(k), 403(b) and governmental 457 employee limit is $24,500, with an $8,000 catch-up at 50 or older (or $11,250 at ages 60 to 63 if the plan allows). The IRA limit is $7,500, or $8,600 at 50 or older. HSA limits are $4,400 for self-only and $8,750 for family coverage, plus $1,000 at 55 or older.

Is an HSA really a retirement account?

Not formally, but it can work like one for healthcare costs. Qualified medical withdrawals are tax-free, and unused balances roll over. You must be covered by an HSA-eligible health plan to contribute, and contributions stop once you enroll in Medicare.
